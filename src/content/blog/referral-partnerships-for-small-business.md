Somebody spoke to your next customer about ninety days before you did.

An accountant told them the business needed better records. An architect drew something that will need building. A recruiter placed somebody who now needs the thing you sell. A realtor handed over keys to a house that needs work. An insurance broker reviewed a policy and found a gap.

Those conversations happened, they happened without you, and the person who had them is the most under-used source of work available to a small service business.

The method is not complicated and it is almost always done wrong. Map who touches your buyer before you do. Then work out what that person is actually risking by mentioning your name, and solve that, because it is not money. And for a surprising number of them, money is not merely the wrong incentive, it is illegal.

## Who touches the customer before you do

Twenty minutes with a pen, and be specific rather than categorical.

Take your last ten customers and work backwards. In the ninety days before they contacted you, which other professionals did they pay or consult? Not who could have. Who did.

For a commercial cleaner: the fit-out contractor, the commercial realtor, the facilities consultant. For a bookkeeper: the attorney who set up the entity, the banker, the insurance agent. For a remodeler: the architect, the realtor, the home inspector.

You will land on four or five names rather than dozens, and the useful ones share two properties: they see the customer earlier than you do, and they are not competing for the same budget.

Then one more pass, which is the step people skip. For each one, write down what they get asked by their own clients that they cannot answer. That gap is where your referral lives. The architect gets asked "who should build this" and most architects hate answering it, for reasons that are the subject of the next section.

## Their currency is not money

Here is the thing that makes most partnership programs fail, and it is a misreading of what the other party cares about.

You offer 10% of the first invoice. They nod politely and never refer anybody.

Think about what they are actually weighing. They have a client relationship worth years of fees. If they recommend you and you are late, rude, or wrong, they do not lose a commission, they lose standing with their own client. The downside is reputational and it is theirs, and it arrives whether or not your work was the problem.

Against that, a few hundred dollars is not a serious offer. It is close to insulting, because it prices their relationship at the value of one job.

**They are not buying a commission. They are buying the certainty of not looking foolish.**

Which tells you what to offer instead. Make it safe. Give them something they can hand over that reflects well on them: a written scope within 24 hours, a named person who will pick up the phone, a commitment that you will tell them first if something goes wrong rather than letting their client tell them. Then prove it on a small job before you ask for a bigger one.

The professionals who refer the most are not the ones with the best incentive. They are the ones who have watched you handle something awkward well.

## For some of them, a fee is illegal

This is the part that almost no advice on referral partnerships mentions, and it matters because several of the most obvious partners for a home services or property-adjacent business sit squarely inside it.

If the referral concerns a settlement service connected to a federally related mortgage loan, paying for it is prohibited. The rule is 12 CFR 1024.14, implementing [Section 8 of RESPA](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/compliance/compliance-resources/mortgage-resources/real-estate-settlement-procedures-act/), and the language is blunt: no person shall give and no person shall accept any fee, kickback or other thing of value pursuant to any agreement or understanding, oral or otherwise, that business incident to a settlement service shall be referred to any person. It states that a referral of a settlement service is not a compensable service, and that a company may not pay another company or its employees for referring settlement service business.

Two details worth knowing before you assume you are clear of it.

"Oral or otherwise" means the handshake version counts. There does not need to be a contract.

And "thing of value" is defined extremely broadly. The regulation's own list includes discounts, salaries, commissions, fees, duplicate payments, stock, distributions of partnership profits, credits for money payable later, and even the opportunity to participate in a money-making program. So the workaround people reach for, giving the partner a discount on their own work instead of cash, is named in the text.

What is permitted is payment for services actually performed, and the rule is explicit that a charge for which no or nominal services are performed is an unearned fee.

Scope matters here and I am not your attorney: this applies to settlement services tied to a federally related mortgage loan, not to every referral in the economy. But if your partner list includes realtors, title companies, mortgage brokers, home inspectors or appraisers, that is the neighborhood you are standing in, and it is worth twenty minutes with a lawyer before you design a commission.

Other professions carry their own restrictions. Healthcare has anti-kickback rules, and state bar rules generally stop attorneys paying for client referrals. If a prospective partner tells you they cannot accept a fee, believe them rather than trying to find a structure that works.

Which is convenient, because the fee was never going to work anyway.

## Go one way first, and for longer than feels fair

The reciprocal instinct kills these relationships before they start. You propose that you will send them work if they send you work, both parties agree enthusiastically, and then both wait.

Send first. Three, over a few months, with no mention of reciprocity. Not a name on a list, an actual introduction with context: "This client needs an architect for a loft conversion in a conservation district, I think that is you."

Two things happen. You find out whether they are any good, which you need to know before letting them near your clients. And you create an obligation nobody had to state, which is more durable than one somebody did.

If after three genuine introductions nothing comes back, that is your answer and it cost you nothing. Move to the next name.

## The ask, and why the written agreement dies

When you do ask, ask for something narrower than a partnership.

Not "do you refer work out?" On how open-ended asks fail I have [written about the customer version of this problem](/blog/customer-referral-program-small-business/), and the mechanism is identical: a vague request produces a vague commitment that nobody has to keep.

Ask instead about a specific recurring situation. "When a client asks you who should do the electrical on a project like this, what do you normally say?" That is answerable, it tells you whether a slot exists, and it reveals who currently occupies it.

And skip the formal reciprocal referral agreement. I have watched several get signed and none produce anything, because they create an obligation to refer regardless of fit, which is the opposite of what makes a referral valuable. The partner who has to send you something sends their worst client. The one who wants to sends their best.

## Before lunch

Forty minutes.

Last ten customers, working backwards: who did they pay or consult in the ninety days before they found you? Write the real names, not the categories.

Pick the three who see the customer earliest. For each, write the question their clients ask that they would rather not answer.

Then make one introduction to one of them this week, for something real, with no mention of what you want back.
