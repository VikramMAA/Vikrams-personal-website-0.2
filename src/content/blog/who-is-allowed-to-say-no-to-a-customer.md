Somebody in your company did say no to that customer.

It was your delivery lead, on a Thursday, six weeks after you'd said yes, in an email that opened with "just to set expectations". By then the client had already given their own board the date.

The refusal happened. It always happens. The only variables are who says it and when, and nobody decided either of those on purpose.

That's the thing I want to put in front of you. The ability to decline is a function, like invoicing or hiring, and in most small companies it has never been assigned to anybody. So it defaults to whoever is closest to the work and latest in the process, which is the worst available combination.

And the reason to fix it isn't operational tidiness. It's that a published limit is the only marketing claim you make that cannot be faked.

## The law already worked this out, for sweaters

There's a federal rule that specifies exactly who has to say no, when, and what the customer gets. It has applied for decades, and almost nobody in services has read it.

[16 CFR Part 435](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-16/chapter-I/subchapter-D/part-435), the Mail, Internet, or Telephone Order Merchandise Rule, makes it "an unfair or deceptive act or practice" for a seller to solicit an order "unless, at the time of the solicitation, the seller has a reasonable basis to expect that it will be able to ship any ordered merchandise to the buyer" within the time stated, or within thirty days if no time is stated.

Before the promise. Not after.

It goes further than that in two ways worth noticing.

If you revise the date, the revised date needs its own reasonable basis. And if you tell the buyer you can't say how long the delay will be, the rule requires that you have a reasonable basis for saying even that, and that you give the buyer the reason for the delay.

Then the part I'd frame and hang on the wall. If the Commission brings an action, a seller's failure to have "records or other documentary proof establishing its use of systems and procedures which assure the shipment of merchandise" on time "will create a rebuttable presumption that the seller lacked a reasonable basis" for the promise.

Read that carefully. The absence of a system is itself treated as evidence that you had no business making the commitment. "We were optimistic" is not a defense. The missing process is the finding.

And when the date breaks, the rule says the seller has to offer the buyer, "clearly and conspicuously and without prior demand", a choice between accepting a delay and canceling for a prompt refund. Without prior demand means you tell them before they chase you.

Now the part that makes this interesting rather than just legal. That rule covers merchandise. If you sell services, none of it applies to you.

So the discipline is a federal requirement when somebody orders a sweater, and a matter of personal temperament when somebody signs a six-figure engagement.

## The same sentence, two opposite meanings

When nobody owns the refusal, it still gets made. You know the mechanics because you've watched them.

A proposal goes out containing a deliverable nobody costed, because it was the thing that unblocked the call. A scope grows across eleven emails, none of which was a change order. A delivery lead learns about a commitment from the client, who mentions it as settled.

But the cost isn't the scope creep, and that's the part people get wrong. The cost is that the identical sentence carries opposite information depending on who says it and when.

"We don't do that, and here's why it wouldn't work for you" said by the person selling, before signature, is a standard. It reads as experience. It makes the rest of the proposal more credible, not less.

"We're not going to be able to do that part" said by the person delivering, in month two, is a shambles. It reads as a company that doesn't know what it sells.

Same content. The timing and the mouth are doing all the work.

## A limit is the only claim you can't fake

Here's why I think this belongs in a marketing conversation rather than an operations one.

Look at the claims on your website. Senior people on every account. Responsive. Partner-level attention. We act like an extension of your team.

Every one of those is free to type. Any competitor can write the same sentence this afternoon at no cost, which is precisely why none of them carries information.

A stated limit is different, because it costs money to make. "We don't take projects under a certain size." "We don't do that platform." "We won't run the campaign without tracking in place first." You can only say those things if you're willing to lose the deals that need them, and the buyer knows that.

That's the whole mechanism. A limit is expensive to assert, therefore it is evidence, and evidence is scarce in a category where everything else is assertion.

The firms with the best reputations in any market tend to have widely known limits. Not because being restrictive is charming. Because over years, the limits were the only part of their marketing anybody could verify.

## What assigning it actually looks like

Three pieces, and it isn't a process document.

**One named person who signs off anything outside standard scope.** Not a committee. And ideally not only the owner, because an organization where every refusal needs the founder is a bottleneck disguised as a standard.

**A standing list of what you don't do, written down, visible to the people selling.** Half of it belongs on your website. The other half belongs in the proposal template, in the section nobody currently fills in.

**The no comes from the same mouth as the yes.** This is the one that fixes the most, and it's almost never how it works. The commitment is made by somebody in sales and withdrawn by somebody in delivery, which is the arrangement most likely to make a reasonable decision look like chaos.

For the sales-to-delivery seam specifically, the artefact that carries this is a handoff written before the deal closes rather than after it, which I set out in [customer onboarding is a marketing problem](/blog/customer-onboarding-marketing/) and won't rebuild here.

## Where I come out

An organization where only the owner can say no hasn't delegated sales. It has delegated optimism.

Here's the test, and it takes a minute. Name your last three refusals. Who said each one, when in the process, and to whom.

If you can't name three, you haven't been declining anything. Which means your limits still exist, because every company has them, and they are being discovered by your customers in month two instead of published by you in week one.

That's not a kinder way to run a business. It just moves the disappointment to the most expensive possible moment.
