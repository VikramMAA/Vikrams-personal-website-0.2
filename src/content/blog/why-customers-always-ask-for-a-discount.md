The call goes like this.

They ask what you do. You tell them. They ask what it costs. You tell them. They ask what you can do on the price.

Now notice what never happened in that exchange. Nobody asked a single question about the work.

That's the whole thing, and it isn't about the buyer being stingy. They went to price first because price was the only item in the conversation that could be compared with anything else. Everything else you said was a claim, and claims from one vendor cannot be set against claims from another. The number can be.

So the request for a discount isn't the start of a negotiation. It's a report on your proposal.

## It isn't culture, and believing that it is costs you money

The standard explanation is Indian price sensitivity, and I've read it in a hundred LinkedIn posts. It's wrong in a way that's worth being precise about, for two reasons.

The first is that it's useless. You cannot change the country you sell in, so an explanation that ends there gives you nothing to do on Monday.

The second is that it isn't true of the same buyer across categories. The founder who spends twenty minutes grinding you down on a Rs 60,000 engagement will approve a software renewal at three times that in a forwarded email, pay a photographer's quote for a family function without a murmur, and never once ask the school to reduce the fee. The reflex is selective. It shows up in some purchases and not others, for the same person, in the same week.

What changes between those purchases isn't their attitude to money. It's how much there is to look at besides the price.

## The one buying system in India with written rules doesn't haggle

Here's the part that surprised me when I went and read it.

India's [General Financial Rules 2017](https://finance.py.gov.in/sites/default/files/gfr2017.pdf) govern how the government buys things, which makes them the most formalised and most openly price-driven purchasing process in the country. Rule 173 says that a "contract should ordinarily be awarded to the lowest evaluated bidder whose bid has been found to be responsive and who is eligible and qualified to perform the contract satisfactorily."

Lowest price wins. Exactly what you'd expect.

Then read the clause above it. "Negotiation with bidders after bid opening must be severely discouraged." Price negotiation is allowed in exceptional circumstances only, and then only with the lowest evaluated responsive bidder.

So the most price-obsessed buying machinery in the country explicitly discourages the thing your buyer did on the phone. It doesn't haggle. It does something harder first: it works out who is eligible, responsive and qualified, and only looks at price among the bidders who got through that gate.

**The lowest price wins, but only among the ones who cleared the gate.**

Your buyer is almost certainly not a government department, and I'm not suggesting the rule applies to them. It's worth knowing because it's the one place where the logic is written down, and what it shows is that even a system designed around price puts a comparability test in front of it.

Your proposal didn't give them anything to put in that test. So there was no gate, and price became the entire evaluation. They weren't being cheap. They were being efficient with the only information you gave them.

## What has to be in the room before the number

Three things, and none of them is a better answer to the discount question. I've written separately about [how to move on price without simply dropping it](/blog/how-to-handle-price-objections-without-discounting/) and about [the posture to decide before the call](/blog/how-to-price-services-in-india/), so this is the earlier problem: making sure the question arrives third instead of first.

**One fact about their business that you found yourself.** Not a compliment about their website. A number. Where they currently rank for the query their customers actually type, how long their site takes to load on a phone over mobile data, how many reviews they have against the nearest competitor in Koramangala, how long it took somebody to reply when you submitted their own enquiry form last Tuesday.

This is the one that does the work, and the reason is mechanical rather than clever. It's the only sentence in your proposal that the other three quotes cannot contain, because they didn't look. A buyer holding four quotes and one fact about their own business is no longer comparing four prices.

**A date, not a deliverable.** "Monthly reporting and ongoing optimisation" is a phrase every quote in the pile contains. "By the 14th you'll have the tracking fixed and I'll tell you how many of last month's enquiries actually came from Google, which right now nobody in your company can answer" is not.

**Something you won't do.** More on this below, because it's the most underused line in Indian B2B.

## The itemised quote is an invitation

If you send eight line items with a rupee figure beside each one and a total at the bottom, you've handed over a menu.

A menu gets edited. That's what menus are for. The buyer will go down the list, find the two items they understand least, and ask what happens if those come off. You then either defend work they've already decided is optional or remove it, and either way you're now negotiating the scope of something they never understood in the first place.

Price the outcome. Describe the scope separately, in prose, as what the work involves rather than as a list of billable components. The total is the same number. What changes is that there's no column to run a pen down.

And on the tax line, I've covered why [GST turns one number into two different prices](/blog/how-to-price-services-in-india/) depending on whether the buyer is registered, so I won't repeat it here, except to say that a confused buyer negotiates harder and GST is the commonest source of that confusion.

## What you refuse tells them more than what you include

Almost no proposal in this market contains a refusal, and it's the cheapest credibility available.

"I'm not going to run Meta ads for this. Your buyers are searching with intent and the budget won't survive being split." Or: "I won't do the Instagram posting. You need three people to see you on Google, not three hundred to scroll past you."

Think about what that sentence does from the other side of the table. A vendor who narrows the work is reporting on experience, because narrowing it costs them revenue and they did it anyway. A vendor who agrees to everything is reporting on availability. Buyers can't verify your case studies, but they can tell those two apart instantly, and they do.

It also gives them something to argue with, which is the actual goal. An argument about whether Meta ads belong in the plan is a conversation about the work. That conversation cannot end in "what can you do on the price", because the price stopped being the only thing on the table.
