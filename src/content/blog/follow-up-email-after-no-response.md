Open the thread you are about to add to.

Three messages from you. No replies. And the one you are writing now will sit underneath that record, where the recipient can see the whole thing.

Which is a problem nobody mentions. The thread is a visible account of them having ignored you twice, and answering it means implicitly acknowledging that. Nobody enjoys doing that, so the easiest available move is to leave it alone for another week.

The content rule here is settled and I am not going to re-argue it. Every touch has to carry something, and "just following up" is a request for attention with nothing offered in return, which I set out [in the context of leads that went quiet](/blog/crm-for-small-business-india/) along with what counts as something and the cadence that works.

This is about the writing. Six specific things, and the first one is where I was wrong until I went looking.

## The obvious fix is contradicted by the only number there is

My instinct was to break the thread. New subject line on the third attempt, fresh slate, none of the accumulated silence visible.

The only quantitative comparison I could find says the opposite. Overloop reports that replies sent inside the existing thread produced somewhere between 1.6 and 2.2 times the reply rate, across 38 customer accounts in 2026.

Treat that carefully: it is a vendor's own figure, from its own customers, with no methodology published beyond the account count. It is also the only measurement on the question I could find, and the broader advice across the sales tooling world runs the same direction, which is to keep early follow-ups in the thread and only break to a fresh subject at the fourth touch or when the angle genuinely changes.

So do not reach for a new subject line as a trick. Reach for it only when you have something different to say, at which point it is not a trick, it is accurate.

The social cost of replying is real. The fix is not cosmetic.

## Remove the need to explain the silence

Here is the actual fix, and it is the most useful thing in this post.

Write the reply you want to receive, before you write the email. Then check how long it is.

If the shortest useful reply is more than a line, you have asked for too much. "Would you be open to a short call to explore whether this might be a fit?" requires them to check a calendar, form a view, and compose a sentence. "Is this still on your list for this quarter, or has it moved?" can be answered with four words.

And notice what the short reply does. Somebody answering in four words never has to account for the three weeks of silence. You have given them a way back into the conversation that does not require an apology, which is exactly what was blocking them.

**A follow-up that can be answered in one word is a follow-up that does not ask them to explain themselves.**

## Ask for less each time

Most people send the same ask four times and read the lack of reply as a problem of persistence.

Make the ask descend.

The first message asks for a call. The second asks a single question they can answer from their phone. The third asks for a yes or no on whether this is still live. The fourth asks permission to close the file.

Each one is cheaper to satisfy than the last, which means you are not repeating a request they have already declined. You are offering a progressively easier way to end the uncertainty, and at some point one of them is cheap enough.

## Four mechanical things

None of these is clever and all of them get broken.

**Shorter than the original.** Always. Most follow-ups are longer than the email they are chasing, because the writer adds justification for writing again. A follow-up longer than the first email reads as anxiety, and anxiety is not persuasive.

**The ask on the last line, alone, as a question.** Not buried mid-paragraph after the context. People skim a chase, and if the skim finds no question there is nothing to act on.

**Count the sentences that start with "I".** "I wanted to check", "I was hoping", "I know you are busy". Those are about your feelings. A follow-up that is mostly first person is a status update on your own patience. Rewrite at least half of them to start with "you" or with the thing itself.

**Forward your own earlier email with one new line above it.** Not a summary of it. The original sits below, so they do not have to find it or remember it, and your new sentence is the only thing to read. It is the lowest-effort format for them and it signals low effort from you, which is appropriate at touch three.

## The close, and why the usual version fails

You will be told to send a breakup email. Most versions of it are a threat in courteous clothing.

"Since I have not heard back, I will assume this is not a priority and close your file" announces a consequence. It is pressure dressed as politeness, and anybody who has worked in sales recognizes it instantly, which means your buyer probably does too.

The tell is the announcement. A genuine offer to stop does not need to inform you of what will happen; it asks.

The honest version is a question that gives them an easy no, and I wrote the wording I would actually use [in the CRM piece](/blog/crm-for-small-business-india/), so I will not duplicate it here. What I will add is why it outperforms everything before it: it asks for a decision rather than for attention, and a decision is a cheaper thing to give. Some of those replies are a no, and a no is a result. It takes a row off a list that was quietly making you feel behind.

One thing to be honest about on all of this. Published reply-rate benchmarks for cold sequences land around three to four percent on some platforms and higher for well-targeted lists, and every figure I found came from a company selling sending software. The direction they agree on, that sequences with a few follow-ups beat a single email, is not controversial. The specific numbers are not worth planning around, so I would measure your own and ignore theirs.

## Before lunch

Twenty minutes, on one live thread.

Open the oldest unanswered thread that still matters. Write the reply you want to get, in as few words as possible. If it runs past a line, change the ask until it does not.

Then write the follow-up to produce that reply. Shorter than your last one, ask on the final line, and no more than half the sentences starting with "I".

Then look at your other stalled threads and find the ones that are past four attempts. Those get the permission-to-close message this week, not another chase, and you will get more replies from that batch than from anything else you send.
