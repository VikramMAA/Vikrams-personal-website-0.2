Take the line at the top of your homepage and put a competitor's name in it.

> Built in Estonia, the world's most advanced digital society.

Now imagine the three other Estonian companies in your category running exactly that sentence. It stays true for all of them. Which means it distinguishes none of them, and a sentence that cannot distinguish you is not positioning. It is scenery.

That is the swap test, and it is the most useful thirty seconds you can spend on a homepage. If a competitor could paste your headline onto their own site without lying, you have not said anything yet.

## The reputation is real, and that is not the argument

I want to be careful here, because the achievement is genuine and dismissing it would be both rude and wrong.

Estonia has more unicorns per head than any country in the world. Skype and Wise came out of a nation of 1.3 million people. The e-government infrastructure works in a way most of Europe still talks about aspirationally. The reputation was earned rather than marketed into existence.

It is a real asset. It helps you hire. It helps with investors. It is a reason a stranger in Munich opens your email rather than archiving it, which is worth something.

What it does not do is answer the question a buyer is actually holding.

## The buyer is not asking where you are from

Look at what the 2026 research on B2B buying actually says people respond to.

Buying decisions are being driven by proof of outcomes rather than promises, with buyers under cost pressure demanding measurable results and validation. Committees have grown to somewhere around eleven to fourteen internal stakeholders on complex purchases. Buyers complete roughly 60% of their journey before they speak to anybody. And assistants are now a serious influence on which vendors make a shortlist at all.

Nothing in that list is moved by provenance. A procurement lead assembling a business case for eleven colleagues cannot write "they are Estonian" in the justification column.

Meanwhile I could find no quantitative evidence that country of origin drives B2B software purchases in any direction. That absence is not proof it does nothing. It is a reasonable signal that it is not the lever people hope it is.

## Why it fails as positioning, specifically

Here is the structural reason, and it generalises well past Estonia.

Country of origin is a claim about you. Positioning has to be a claim about the buyer's outcome.

And anything true of your entire country is by definition not a differentiator within it. You have selected a fact that is maximally shared with the competitors closest to you. It would be like a Tartu restaurant advertising that it is in Estonia.

**If your strongest line is true of every company in your postcode, you have not positioned, you have located.**

The same trap catches "founded by engineers", "we are remote first", "AI powered" and most of what appears on European SaaS homepages this year.

## The one version that genuinely works

Be fair, because there is a real exception.

Provenance becomes a buyer outcome when it answers a question they were going to ask anyway. EU data residency is the clearest case. "Your data stays in the EU, hosted in Tallinn, under EU law" is not a flag on a homepage. It is a compliance answer that removes a specific objection from a specific buyer's file, and in regulated industries it can decide the deal.

Notice what happened there. The fact did not change. It stopped being about you and started being about their problem.

That is the whole conversion. Every piece of provenance you want to use should survive the same translation, and if it cannot, it belongs in the About page rather than the headline.

## What replaces it

A claim your competitor could not copy without it becoming false.

That is the entire specification, and it is harder than it sounds because it requires knowing something specific about who you serve and what changes for them.

The recipe: a specific outcome, for a specific kind of buyer, with a number or a mechanism attached.

So instead of:

> The modern platform for logistics operations, built in Estonia.

Something closer to:

> Dispatch planning for Baltic hauliers running 10 to 50 vehicles. Two of our customers cut planning time by about a third in the first quarter.

The second one is smaller, duller and much harder to argue with. It also excludes people, which is the part founders resist and the part that makes it work. A haulier with four trucks now knows this is not for them, and you have stopped wasting both your time.

If that register feels uncomfortably modest, it is the correct one for most of your neighbouring markets, though [it needs inverting for American buyers](/blog/selling-saas-to-us-customers-from-europe/), who expect you to state the conclusion rather than let them draw it.

## The new reason this matters more than it used to

Your homepage is increasingly read by a machine assembling a shortlist for somebody who asked a question in an assistant.

A vague provenance claim gives that system nothing to work with. It cannot recommend you for anything in particular, because you did not say what you are for in particular.

A specific outcome claim is quotable. It gives the answer engine a sentence to lift when somebody asks who does dispatch planning for mid sized Baltic fleets, and that is now a real path to being shortlisted. I went through [how these systems decide who to mention](/blog/should-i-block-gptbot/) separately, and the practical implication for positioning is blunt: whatever is too vague for a human to repeat at a meeting is also too vague for a machine to cite.

## The uncomfortable bit

Most companies lean on where they are from because they have not decided who they are for.

Provenance is available, it is flattering, nobody argues with it, and it requires no choices. Positioning requires choosing a buyer, which means choosing who you are prepared to lose, and that decision is genuinely frightening when revenue is thin.

But the alternative is a homepage that could belong to anybody, sitting in a market where buyers do most of their evaluation without you and where committees need something concrete to put in a document.

Being from a small country with a good reputation is a nice thing to have and a poor thing to lead with. I made a related argument about [distribution being the harder half of the Estonian problem](/blog/estonian-startup-marketing/), and this is the same discipline aimed at the first sentence a stranger reads.

## Do this today

Open your homepage. Put your nearest competitor's name into your headline and read it back.

If it survives, rewrite it until it would not, using an outcome, a buyer type and a number.

Then find every sentence on the page that describes you rather than what changes for them, and either translate it or move it down the page.

Keep the Estonian part. Put it in the footer with your registry number, where it does honest work as reassurance rather than pretending to be a reason to buy.
