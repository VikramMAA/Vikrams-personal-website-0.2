Search your own company name on your phone, signed out, and count what sits above your website.

Their ad. Possibly an AI summary. Possibly a directory listing that has outranked you for years. Then you.

The competitor's ad is one of three or four things pushing your own site down the page, and it's the only one you noticed, because it's the only one that feels personal. Worth knowing before you respond, because it changes what you're actually buying.

Here's the short answer. Google will not stop a competitor using your brand name as a keyword. It may well stop them using your brand name inside the ad text. Those are two different problems: the second one has a complaint form and takes twenty minutes, and the first one is an economic decision that most owners get wrong in the direction of spending money.

## What Google will and won't do about it

Most advice on this is simply wrong, so read the actual policy rather than a summary.

[Google's trademark policy](https://support.google.com/adspolicy/answer/6118) does not restrict the use of trademarks as keywords. Not on appeal, not with a registration, not ever. Anyone can bid on your name and Google considers that a matter between you and them.

What it does restrict is your trademark appearing **in the ad itself**, where the advertiser is a direct competitor, or where the use is confusing, deceptive or misleading. The policy is explicit that for review purposes the trademark has to be in the ad, not only on the landing page. There are carve-outs: resellers whose landing page clearly offers the product for sale with prices, and informational sites about the trademarked product.

Three details decide whether a complaint gets you anywhere, and nobody mentions them.

Google accepts complaints against **specific advertisers identified by URL**, and only in the countries and industries where you have shown you hold trademark rights. If you've never registered the mark, you start from a weak position however long you've traded under the name.

A restriction, once granted, generally applies to ads using the **same second-level domain** in their final URL. So the remedy is scoped to a domain rather than a company, and a determined competitor can run the same ad from another one.

And a trademark sitting in the second-level domain of their display URL is **not** restricted. If their domain contains your brand name, that part is not a policy problem.

So: if their ad copy contains your name, screenshot it with the ad's URL, get your registration number and classes together, and file today. If it doesn't, there is nothing to file, and the rest of this is the real question.

## Work out what it's actually costing you

Two reports, twenty minutes.

**Auction insights**, on your own brand campaign if you run one. Look at overlap rate and position above rate for that competitor specifically. This tells you how often they show when you do, and how often they're above you.

**Search Console**, filtered to your brand query. Pull clicks, impressions and click-through rate for the last 90 days and compare against the 90 before. This is the number that matters, and it's the one nobody checks.

Why does it matter more than the auction report?

Because if your organic click-through rate on your own name hasn't moved, you're looking at an insult rather than a loss. People searching your company name are navigating, not shopping, and they are remarkably good at scrolling past an ad that isn't you.

There's real research on this, worth knowing before you spend anything. Blake, Nosko and Tadelis ran a controlled field experiment at eBay, published in Econometrica in 2015, where paid search was switched off across a sample of US geographies. For brand keywords they found no measurable short-term benefit: the paid link was intercepting people at the last step of a journey they'd have finished anyway, and the clicks moved to the free organic result.

Now the caveat that makes your situation different from eBay's. It's the whole thing.

In that experiment, nobody was sitting above eBay. Substitution from paid to organic works when the rest of the page still belongs to you. It stops working when the first thing a customer sees is somebody else, or when an AI summary has pushed your own result below the fold on a phone. So the research tells you what a defensive brand campaign is worth in the normal case. Your Search Console numbers tell you whether you're in the normal case any more.

If your brand CTR has fallen and their overlap rate is high, you have a measured problem. If your brand CTR is flat, you have an annoyance, and buying clicks you were getting free is the most common way this ends badly.

## If you do run a defensive campaign, run it properly

Keep it narrow, because the point is insurance rather than volume.

- Exact match on your brand name and its close variants, including the common misspellings. Nothing else in the campaign.
- A separate brand-only campaign, so brand clicks never get mixed into the numbers you use to judge everything else.
- Negatives for every service and product term, so it can't quietly start absorbing generic traffic at brand CPCs.
- A capped daily budget rather than an impression share target. Chasing 100% impression share on your own name is how a small insurance policy becomes a line item.

One thing to check before you build it. Your own Performance Max campaign may already be serving on your company name and charging you for clicks you'd have had anyway, a different problem with the same symptom, which I went through [what Performance Max is actually doing with your budget](/blog/performance-max-explained/) separately.

Your quality score on your own name should be excellent, because you are by some distance the most relevant advertiser for it. If your cost per click on your own brand term isn't low, the match types are wrong or the ad group is carrying terms that don't belong in it.

## The one thing not to do is call them and agree to stop

This is the obvious fix and it's the one with a history.

Between 2004 and 2013 the contact lens retailer 1-800 Contacts settled thirteen trademark disputes with competitors. Each agreement prohibited the parties from using each other's trademarks, URLs and variations as search keywords, and required them to employ negative keywords so a search for one party's name would not trigger the other's ads. Generic terms like "contact lenses" were left alone.

The FTC found that those agreements violated Section 5 of the FTC Act. 1-800 Contacts appealed, and on 11 June 2021 the Second Circuit vacated the order, holding that while trademark settlement agreements are not immune from antitrust scrutiny, the Commission had applied the wrong standard.

Read what that leaves standing. The agreements that survived were settlements of genuine trademark disputes, and the court said plainly that such agreements are not immune from scrutiny. A phone call between two rivals who find the auction expensive is not that. It's two competitors agreeing where they will and won't compete, which is a worse story in front of a regulator and an easy one to tell badly in an email.

I'm not your lawyer and this isn't advice. The point is narrower: of all the responses available to you, this is the only one where you want to talk to a lawyer before you talk to the competitor.

## Before lunch

Three things, in this order.

Search your name signed out on a phone and screenshot the whole page, because that's your evidence and your baseline. Open Search Console and compare your brand query's CTR across the last 90 days and the 90 before. And if your name appears in their ad copy, file the trademark complaint today, since that one is free and actually works.
