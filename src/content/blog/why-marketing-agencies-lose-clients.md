The most expensive thing that will happen to your agency this quarter is a LinkedIn notification.

Your main contact has started a new role.

Nothing about your work changed that morning. The campaigns are running, the numbers are what they were on Friday, and the account is now in more danger than any underperforming quarter has ever put it.

That is the uncomfortable shape of agency churn. Most of it is not a verdict on the work. It is personnel and it is calendars, and because agencies diagnose every loss as a performance problem, the standard response is to do more work, which is the one response that does not help.

The thing that actually protects an account is coverage. More relationships, held by more of your people, with more of theirs. That is unglamorous, it is nobody's favorite project, and it is worth more than another round of optimization.

## The number that should change your account reviews

There is one statistic here worth knowing, with a caveat about where it comes from.

Research by Sturdy, circulated widely through ChurnZero, puts the chance of an account churning within twelve months of an unmanaged champion change at **51%**. Where the person leaving was the executive sponsor, the twelve month non-renewal figure rises to about **65%**.

The caveat: this traces back to a 2025 vendor presentation and I could not get to the underlying study, and most sites quoting it add nothing. So hold the decimal places loosely. What I would trust is the direction and the rough magnitude, because it matches what anybody who has run accounts has watched happen.

Set it against what agencies actually measure. Almost every account health review I have seen scores delivery, results and responsiveness. Hardly any of them has a line for how many people at the client know who you are.

**Your single biggest renewal risk is a fact about their org chart, and it is probably not in your CRM.**

On the agency side of the ledger, the best-sourced figure I found comes from Promethean Research, who surveyed 165 digital agency leaders: 42% reported average retainer tenures above two years, while 24% said their typical client leaves inside a year. You will also see a cleaner-looking split quoted, something like 18% annual churn for retainer agencies against 42% for project-based ones. It traces to a single 2026 report that other sites recirculate without checking, so I am mentioning it rather than leaning on it.

## The four dates

None of these is a results event. All of them are predictable, which is what makes the failure to prepare for them strange.

**Your champion leaves.** Covered above. The replacement inherits a vendor they did not choose, a contract they did not negotiate, and a predecessor whose decisions they have no stake in defending.

**The budget cycle.** Not the renewal date, the planning cycle that precedes it, usually one to two quarters earlier. The decision to cut your line happens in a document being drafted while you are still delivering normally. By the time anybody tells you, the number has been in a spreadsheet for weeks.

**They hire someone in-house.** This is the one most agencies read as a straightforward threat and misread badly. The new marketing manager does not need to cut you to justify their salary; what they need is a visible win that is theirs. If your work is the thing they can point at, you are safe for years. If your work is a black box they inherited, you are the obvious thing to replace with something they built.

**A reorg or an acquisition.** Here your sponsor may still be there and no longer have the authority they had. Procurement arrives, every vendor gets reviewed on paper, and the quality of the relationship stops mattering because the people deciding have never met you.

What each one needs is different, and only the first two can be handled with a calendar invite.

## Why agencies are structurally blind to this

Here is the part I think is the real mechanism, and it is uncomfortable for people in my line of work.

Agencies model churn as a constant. Twenty, thirty percent a year, baked into the financial plan, so the business is built to replace clients rather than to keep them. Once that assumption is in the model, the behavior follows it automatically.

New business gets the senior people, because new business is where growth is measured. Existing accounts get whoever is available, which over eighteen months means progressively more junior. The client notices a change they often cannot articulate: the calls feel thinner, the recommendations feel more generic, nobody is arguing with them any more.

Then they leave, and the churn assumption is confirmed, and the model is rewarded for being right.

I am not describing anybody's bad intentions. It is an entirely rational response to how agency P&Ls are built, and it is self-fulfilling in a way that is hard to see from inside, because every individual staffing decision was defensible on the day it was made.

## What clients should be demanding

If you are on the buying side, this tells you what to ask for, and it is not more reporting.

Ask who specifically is on your account and how many hours a week. Then ask again in month nine, and compare. The drift is the thing, not the starting answer.

Ask for the agency to be in front of more than one person at your company. If your agency only knows you, then you are a single point of failure for your own supplier, and when you move roles your successor will inherit a relationship with nobody in it.

And give them the thing they cannot get on their own, which is early notice of your budget cycle. An agency that knows your planning happens in August can bring you something to defend the line with in July. One that finds out in October is negotiating a decision that has already been written down.

## The version of this that applies to me

Fairness requires saying that independent consultants have a worse version of this problem, not a better one.

An agency with thinning seniority still has a firm behind it. A solo consultant is one relationship, full stop. If the person who hired me moves on, there is no account director with a separate line into the business, no second pair of hands who has met the team. The coverage argument is the strongest structural case for hiring a firm instead of a person, and I would rather say that plainly than pretend my setup has no downside.

What the independent can offer against it is that the senior person on the account in month twenty is the same one who was there in week one, because there is nobody to hand it to. Whether that trade is worth it depends on the work, and anybody who tells you it is always worth it is selling.

## Where I come out

Go and look at why you lost your last three accounts, then go and look at who was still in their job on the day the decision was made.

My guess is that at least one of those losses had a personnel change in front of it that nobody logged as the cause, and that the post-mortem was about the work instead.

Fix the coverage problem and you will keep accounts whose results were merely fine. Fix nothing and you will keep losing accounts whose results were good, and you will keep concluding that the work was the issue.
