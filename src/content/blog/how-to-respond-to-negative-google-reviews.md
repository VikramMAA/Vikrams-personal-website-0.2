Same one star review. Two replies. Read both and decide which business you would call.

> "We have no record of any appointment under this name. Our technicians are all background checked and we have been serving this area for 22 years with a 4.8 rating. We suggest you check whether you have the correct company."

> "You're right that we quoted Tuesday and showed up Thursday, and nobody called you in between. That's on our scheduling, not on you. We've moved to same-day text confirmations since. I'd like to make it right if you'll let me. Dave, owner, 555-0142."

The first one wins the argument. The second one gets the next customer, and the next customer is the only person whose opinion is still available to you.

That is the entire discipline here, and it is harder than it sounds because the first reply is what you actually want to write at 9pm on the day the review lands.

## Write it for the person reading in March

The reviewer has moved on. You are not going to change their mind, and the handful of cases where you do are not worth optimizing for.

The audience is the prospect who finds you in six months, sorts your reviews by lowest first, which a surprising number of people do, and reads how you behaved when something went wrong. That is the only place on your profile where they get to see your character rather than your marketing.

I set out the basic four part structure in [the piece on getting more reviews](/blog/how-to-get-more-google-reviews/). What that post did not do is give you the actual sentences, so here are three, for the three situations that need genuinely different handling.

## Reply one: you got it wrong

The easiest to write and the one businesses overthink most.

> "You're right. We quoted $340 and invoiced $520 without calling you first, and that isn't how we should work. I've refunded the difference. We now confirm any change over $50 by phone before doing it. I'm sorry, and thank you for saying it plainly. Maria, owner."

Four things doing work there. Specific agreement, so the reader knows you read it. A number, so it is clearly a real incident and not a template. A change that resulted, which is the part that reassures. A name.

No "we strive to", no "we take all feedback seriously", no paragraph about your values.

## Reply two: they are partly wrong on the facts

This is where most businesses lose, because being right feels like it should help.

> "Thanks for taking the time. Our record shows the delivery signed for on the 14th, so I think something has gone wrong between us that I'd like to understand rather than debate here. Can you call me on 555-0142 and ask for Sam? I'll dig through it with you either way."

Notice what it does not do. It does not say you are mistaken. It does not attach evidence. It states your position once, in one clause, then moves to the offer.

The reader in March does not know who is right and will not adjudicate. What they notice is which party stayed calm. Correcting somebody publicly reads as defensiveness even when the correction is accurate, and there is no version of a public argument that you win.

## Reply three: you cannot discuss it at all

Clinics, law firms, therapists, anyone with a confidentiality obligation, and in some sectors acknowledging that somebody was a client is itself a breach.

> "Thank you for the feedback. We aren't able to discuss anything about specific visits in a public forum, so I don't want to say more here. If you'd like to talk it through, our practice manager is on 555-0142 and will make time this week."

That is the whole reply. It is short on purpose. Every additional sentence in this category is a chance to confirm something you should not have confirmed, and "we have no record of you" is the exact sentence that has cost practices real money.

## What Google will actually remove

Set expectations first, because the removal route is slower and narrower than most people expect.

Google removes content that is not based on a real experience or does not accurately represent the place. So: invented visits, reviews written by somebody with a stake in the business, coached or incentivized reviews, and anything hitting the prohibited content rules like harassment, personal information or off topic ranting.

Google does not remove a review because it is unfair, because the person was difficult, or because you disagree with their account. Being wrong is not against the policy.

To report one, open the review in your Business Profile, use the flag or report option, and pick the specific policy it breaks rather than the generic complaint. Then wait, and expect the first answer to be no more often than not.

The honest counsel is to spend eighty percent of your effort on the reply and twenty on the removal request. A calm reply is available immediately, is under your control, and works on the reader whether or not the review ever disappears.

## The April 2026 change that catches good businesses

This is the part worth acting on today, because it made a widespread and well intentioned tactic a violation.

Under Google's rating manipulation policy, staff review quotas and asking customers to mention an employee by name are now explicit violations.

Read that twice if you run a home services, dental or salon business, because "if you were happy, please mention Dave in your review" is standard practice in those trades. It is now on the wrong side of the line.

Employee reviews are not banned outright, but the person has to disclose that they work there and it has to describe a genuine experience. The five star review from your office manager written in the voice of a delighted customer is the thing being targeted.

The penalties escalate rather than arriving all at once. Google can block your profile from receiving new reviews, temporarily unpublish the reviews you already have, and place a public banner on your profile telling people that fake reviews were found and removed. Suspension sits at the end of that ladder for repeated or severe cases.

Sit with the banner one for a second. Every consequence in that list is recoverable except the impression left on everyone who sees a notice on your listing saying fake reviews were removed.

One thing worth knowing if you have been the victim rather than the cause: a single fake review from a third party does not put your profile at risk. What Google acts on is a pattern, particularly coordinated posting or reviews traced to the same source.

## Before lunch

Three things, about thirty minutes.

Ask whoever collects your reviews whether they say anything about naming staff, and whether anyone has a monthly target. If either is true, stop it today and put the reason in writing so it stays stopped.

Then open your profile, sort by lowest rating, and find every negative review with no reply. Write one for each using whichever of the three patterns above fits. Old reviews still count, and an unanswered one star from last year is doing damage right now.

Last, read your own worst review as a stranger would, including your reply if there is one, and ask whether you would call that business.
