Sales had more than tripled in five years, to nearly three billion dollars.

Then the company posted its first loss in decades.

That's a real case, and Robert Kaplan uses it as his opening example in [a piece on customer profitability for Harvard Business School](https://www.library.hbs.edu/working-knowledge/a-balanced-scorecard-approach-to-measure-customer-profitability). A large distributor of medical and surgical supplies, growing fast, margins down a full percentage point, in the red. His diagnosis: the selling, general and administrative costs everybody had filed under "fixed" had turned out to be, in his words, "super-variable."

Your version of this is smaller and it is the same mechanism. And the document that hides it is probably open on your screen right now.

It's your client list, ranked by revenue. Almost every service business has one and nothing else, and it tells you close to nothing about which of those clients make you money. Worse, the thing you do once you know is not a firing decision. It's an acquisition brief, and that's the part nobody gets to.

## Why the revenue list feels trustworthy

Kaplan names the two ways companies get this wrong, and the second one is the interesting one. Either they treat indirect costs "as fixed-period costs and don't drive them to the customer level", or they use "high-level, inaccurate methods, such as allocating a flat percentage of sales revenue to each customer to cover 'below-the-line' indirect expenses."

Sit with that second method for a second, because most small firms use it without noticing.

If you allocate your overhead to each client as a flat percentage of what that client pays you, then your profit ranking is arithmetically identical to your revenue ranking. The biggest account is also the most profitable, by construction. You haven't measured anything. You've re-sorted the same list and given it a new column heading.

That's exactly why it feels right. It's internally consistent, it's reproducible, and it's completely uninformative.

## What the real distribution looks like

Here's the number worth knowing, in Kaplan's own words: the shape of the cumulative profitability curve "occurs in virtually every customer profitability study ever done, in which 15 percent to 20 percent of the customers generate 100 percent (or more) of the profits."

He gives one worked case, an insurance company that ranked its customers once it knew its real cost of serving them. The most profitable 40 percent generated 130 percent of annual profits. The middle 55 percent broke even. The least profitable 5 percent lost the company an amount equal to 30 percent of annual profits.

Read that bottom line again. Five percent of the customer base was consuming almost a third of the year's profit.

Now the honest caveat, because these are large companies with distribution networks and you may have eleven people. There's a wider version of this statistic circulating, that the top 20 percent produce 150 to 300 percent of profits, and I've only been able to find it in secondary sources, so I'm not going to quote it at you. Kaplan's own formulation is enough.

The reason the pattern transfers down to a small firm isn't scale. It's that in a service business the cost that varies by customer is time, and time is the only thing you sell. There's nowhere for it to hide.

## The things that cost you money are all reasonable

Kaplan lists what companies give away while capturing additional business: "product or service customization; small order quantities; special packaging; expedited and just-in-time delivery; substantial pre-sales support from marketing, technical, and sales resources; extra post-sales support for installation, training, warranty, and field service; and liberal payment terms."

Translate that into your week.

The client who needs the report in their format. The one who books a call rather than replying to an email. The one whose procurement process adds six weeks to every change order. The one who pays at sixty days. The one with three stakeholders who each have to be convinced separately, in separate meetings, about the same decision.

Every one of those creates genuine value for that client. Not one of them is an unreasonable request. None of them is in the price.

Kaplan's sentence about it is the one I'd put on the wall: these services "create value and loyalty among customers," but "none of them come for free."

## The method is two numbers

You don't need a costing system. Kaplan's own simplified approach, which he calls time-driven activity-based costing, needs exactly two inputs: the cost per hour of each group of people doing the work, and the time their activities actually take. His example is a support department at $70 an hour and a transaction that takes 24 minutes, so the transaction costs $28.

For a small firm that's an afternoon's work. Loaded cost per hour by role, including the overhead you'd otherwise smear across revenue. Then two weeks of honest time logging per client, counting the parts nobody logs: the Slack monitoring, the internal meeting about their internal meeting, the proposal revision that was really account management.

Be clear with yourself that the number will be rough. Rough is fine, because you're ranking, not filing accounts. You only need enough precision to see which clients are on the wrong side of the line.

## Notice what isn't on Kaplan's list of remedies

Having found the unprofitable accounts, he does not say get rid of them. He says three other things first, in order.

**Look at your own processes before you look at the client**, because "we can't expect customers to pay for our inefficiencies." If a client has drifted to smaller, more frequent requests, the first question is what each request costs you to handle, not whether they're allowed to make them.

**Put the variations on a menu and price them.** A base price for the standard thing with standard delivery and standard terms, and a priced option for every departure from it, each one priced to at least cover what it costs. His point about this is subtle and good: the menu prices also change what clients ask for, in a direction that's cheaper for both of you.

**Widen a thin relationship rather than repricing it.** A client who buys one narrow service is often unprofitable because of the fixed cost of having them at all, and the fix may be selling them more rather than charging more for the one thing.

Firing them isn't on the list. It's available, and I've written about when it's right, but it's fourth.

## The part that makes this marketing rather than accounting

This is where I'd push you, because almost everybody treats customer profitability as a finance exercise and files the answer.

Once you know which shape of client makes money, you know what to go and buy. Not a vertical, a shape: the size, the sector, how they decide, how many people have to agree, how they pay, whether they want the standard thing.

And here's the trap, which is the whole reason this is urgent. The revenue list flatters your worst cohort. So your case studies get written about those accounts, because they're the big names. Your testimonials come from them. Your targeting, your service page, the examples in your proposals, all of it quietly describes the clients who lose you money, because the list you trust most is the one that put them at the top.

You are spending your acquisition budget on more of the wrong cohort, and the document telling you to do it looks like a spreadsheet of good news.

A firm that cannot name its most profitable customer type doesn't have an acquisition strategy. It has an acquisition budget.
