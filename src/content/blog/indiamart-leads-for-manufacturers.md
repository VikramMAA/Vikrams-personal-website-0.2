There are two completely different things arriving in your IndiaMART inbox, and most sellers treat them identically.

One is a direct enquiry. A buyer found your catalogue, looked at your products, and contacted you. That enquiry is yours.

The other is a BuyLead. A buyer posted a requirement into a pool, and you spend a credit from your plan to unlock their details. So can other suppliers.

Those two need opposite behaviour, and conflating them is why the package arithmetic never works out the way the sales call said it would. A direct enquiry rewards a good reply. A BuyLead is a race you have paid to enter, and your position in that race is decided by who is sitting next to a phone, not by which plan you bought.

Which gives you the short version. Fix your answering before you upgrade your plan, and fix your listing fields before you do either.

## What is actually attested, and what is not

Worth separating, because a lot of confident mechanics get repeated about this platform.

IndiaMART's own help documentation does not explain how its allocation algorithm picks which suppliers see which requirement. It also does not publish a response-time rule, despite the five-minute window you will see quoted. The figures floating about on how many suppliers receive each enquiry come from software vendors rather than from the platform.

What is not in dispute is the structure. A BuyLead sits in a pool and is unlocked with a credit, which means more than one supplier can be holding the same buyer's number at the same time. You do not need an algorithm diagram to work out what that does to your position.

And the consequence is the thing the exporters version of this argument already covers: [a marketplace listing is a commodity position by construction](/blog/seo-for-exporters-india/), because you arrive beside forty suppliers with near-identical copy in a layout where price is the only visible difference. For export work my advice was to keep the listing and stop treating it as the channel. Domestically it is a different story, because this is what the platform is genuinely good at, so the question becomes how to work inside that constraint rather than whether to.

## The allocations, which are published

Here is the part you can actually calculate with, and it comes from [IndiaMART's own help page on BuyLead allocation](https://help.indiamart.com/knowledge-base/buy-lead-allocation), updated in March 2026.

BuyLeads per week, by plan: the entry-level Mini Dynamic tier at 7 on the monthly version or 10 on the annual, Trustseal Pro at 14, Maximiser Pro at 21, IM Star Pro at 30, IM Leader Pro at 40, and IM IL at 70. Each of those also carries a daily bonus BuyLead, one for most plans and two at the top. The three largest plans get additional category-wise weekly leads on top, and the page does not say how many.

So a Maximiser Pro plan is roughly 21 plus 7, call it 28 BuyLeads a week and around 120 a month.

Now do the sum with your own quoted price, because package pricing is negotiated and I am not going to quote you a figure somebody will undercut. Take the monthly cost, divide by 120, and that is what you are paying per unlocked contact. Then apply the hit rate: in my experience and in what sellers consistently report, somewhere between a third and a half of paid leads turn into a conversation at all. So your real cost per conversation is two to three times the per-lead number.

One sanity check from the platform's own reporting, which I used [when writing about export channels](/blog/seo-for-exporters-india/): annualised revenue per paying supplier was around Rs 69,000, so roughly Rs 5,750 a month. If you are being pitched something far above that, you are being sold the top of a market whose average participant spends a fraction of it.

## A bigger plan buys more races, not more orders

Here is the test I would run before any upgrade conversation, and it takes no spreadsheet.

An enquiry lands at 7.40pm on a Saturday. What happens to it?

If the answer is that somebody sees it on Monday morning, your conversion problem is not a volume problem and no plan fixes it. You would be buying more entries into races that get decided before you arrive. More credits, same orders, higher cost per order.

So the sequence is unglamorous. Get the enquiries routed to a phone that a human is holding, including evenings and Sundays, because industrial buyers work Saturdays and the cheapest advantage available on this platform is being awake. WhatsApp is the right surface for it, since that is where the conversation is going to continue anyway.

Then decide who owns the first reply, by name. "Whoever sees it" means nobody.

Only after that does the plan size become the binding constraint, and at that point upgrading is a reasonable decision rather than a hopeful one.

## The listing fields that do the filtering

Four fields earn their place, and what they have in common is that they lose you enquiries.

That is the point. The enquiries they lose are the ones you were going to lose anyway, after forty minutes on the phone.

**Minimum order quantity.** The highest-filtering field on the page, and the one most sellers leave blank because every enquiry feels like a chance. Stating it costs you the buyer who wanted 50 units when you start at 1,000, and that buyer was never your customer.

**Grade and specification, in the buyer's vocabulary.** The standard, the tolerance, the material grade, the certification. A serious buyer scans for this and a casual one does not read it, which is exactly the sorting you want.

**Manufacturer or trader, stated plainly.** Buyers check this first, and being caught is considerably worse than being a trader. If you source rather than make, say so and sell the thing you are actually good at, which is usually consolidation and reliability.

**Lead time, honestly.** Including the busy-season version. A supplier who says fourteen days in November and twenty-one in March reads as somebody who runs a real shop floor.

## The reply that stops it being a price comparison

The bidding war is not imposed on you by the platform. It happens in your first message.

A buyer collecting quotes does so because none of the eleven suppliers gave him enough to decide with, so the only tool left is comparison on the one number everybody provided. If your reply is "kindly share your detailed requirement", you have handed him that tool and joined a queue.

The reply that breaks the pattern gives a price with its conditions attached, in the first message. Rs 142 per unit at 500 units, Rs 128 at 2,000, ex-works, dispatch in twelve working days, GST extra. Then one question that only a supplier who understands the application would ask, and one specific next step with a time on it.

Three things happen. You have answered the question he actually had. You have made the number conditional, so comparing it to a bare figure from somebody else requires him to think about volume and terms rather than price alone. And you have demonstrated, in four lines, that you know what you are doing.

If quoting a number in writing before qualifying feels wrong, notice that the alternative is competing on a number you refused to name, chosen by somebody else's quote.

## Before lunch

Forty minutes, and the first two matter most.

Open your three best-selling product listings and fill in minimum order quantity, grade or specification, and lead time. If you are a trader, make sure the page says so.

Then route enquiry alerts to a phone somebody is actually holding on a Saturday, and write one name against "who replies first".

Then draft the four-line reply template: conditioned price, one intelligent question, one next step with a time. You will use it this week.
