Since July, your job advert has had a salary range in it. So has everybody else's.

Which means a candidate comparing you with one of the large Tallinn employers knows the size of the gap before either of you has spoken, and nothing you say in an interview is going to close it.

That is better news than it sounds. It just requires you to have something to say second.

And here is the problem with what most twelve-person companies say second. Ownership. Real impact. Flat structure, no politics, your work actually matters. All of it is probably true at your company and none of it will do any work, because it is free to say, it is unverifiable, and the candidate has read the identical paragraph three times this month from three other founders who also meant it.

Claims that cost nothing to make get discounted to nothing. So the only thing left that moves a candidate is an artefact: something they can read, check, or be shown.

## What the law actually changed, which most founders have not read

Worth being precise, because the recruitment-stage rules are in force and the rest is not.

Estonia transposed the recruitment part of the EU pay transparency directive and left the rest. Per the [Ministry of Economic Affairs' own page](https://mkm.ee/en/work-and-equal-opportunities/employment-relationships-and-work-environment/pay-transparency), the amendments to the Employment Contracts Act entered into force on 13 July 2026, and they require that applicants get pay information in writing no later than before the job interview. In the advert, or by email or SMS if the advert leaves it out. The range has to be realistic and based on basic pay, and collectively agreed bonuses or supplements have to be disclosed too.

Two more things in there that nobody has told you about.

Questions about a candidate's previous or current pay are prohibited. And an employer must not prevent an employee from disclosing their own pay, which covers contract clauses and informal discouragement alike.

The heavier obligations, the pay structure criteria, the equal-value pay information on request, the gender pay gap reporting at 100 and 250 employees, are not currently in legislative procedure. So for a company your size, the recruitment-stage rules are effectively the whole of it.

Now the consequence. You have lost two things you used to rely on without noticing. You can no longer get a candidate into the room before money comes up, and you can no longer anchor an offer on what they currently earn, because you are not allowed to ask.

Both of those were crutches. Losing them forces the conversation you should have been having.

## Why the big employer is not the villain here

A thing to get right, because getting it wrong makes you sound bitter and candidates can hear that immediately.

At two thousand people a role genuinely is narrower. That is a property of scale rather than a failing, and the same scale is what pays for the salary, the stability and the training budget. Those companies are good places to work, and a large share of the sector's experienced people are inside them, which I went through when writing about [who to hire first here](/blog/first-marketing-hire-estonia/).

The candidate already knows all of that, which is precisely why you do not need to tell them their current employer is bureaucratic. What you are offering is a different trade, not a better version of the same one, and the moment you frame it as a better version you have invited a comparison you lose on every measurable axis.

## Replace the claim with something checkable

Here is the part worth doing this month. Five artefacts, in rough order of how much they move people.

**A decision that went badly, and what happened after.** This is the strongest one and almost nobody does it. "Real influence" is only believable if a candidate can see influence being exercised imperfectly by somebody who still works there. A company that publishes only wins reads as a company where nobody's judgement is actually visible, which reads as a company where judgement is not actually delegated. One honest post about a thing you got wrong does more than a year of culture content.

**Names, with what each person decides without asking.** Not a grid of photographs. One sentence per person describing the decisions they make alone. A candidate reading that can locate themselves in it, which is the thing you are actually selling.

**The numbers you would show in week one, shown now.** Revenue shape, customer count, growth, whatever you would put in front of them on day three anyway. If showing it feels impossible, then "your work will be visible in the numbers" is a promise you cannot keep, and you should stop making it.

**Where the last two people who left went, and roughly why.** Risky, and that is the point. An alumni line is the most credible employer signal available and small companies never publish one, because it means admitting people leave. Everybody already assumes they do.

**The first ninety days, written as ownership rather than onboarding.** Not "you will learn our systems". A list of what this person owns by day ninety and who they will have to say no to.

Put these on the careers page, not in the advert. The advert gets skimmed and compared on the number. The careers page is where somebody goes after they have decided you are interesting enough to check, and it is the page almost every small company treats as an afterthought.

## Name the gap yourself

Since the range is published anyway, say the obvious thing out loud instead of letting them do the arithmetic in silence.

A larger employer will pay more. Here is what you get instead, specifically. Here is the person who should not take this job.

That last sentence is the one that works, and it is the one founders cannot bring themselves to write. Somebody with a mortgage, a young family and a low appetite for variance is not making a mistake by taking the salary and the stability, and telling them so plainly costs you a candidate you were going to lose anyway while buying you the only thing that scales in a market of 1.3 million people, which is being spoken about accurately by people who did not join you.

One honest cost, because I am not trying to sell you the idea. This pitch selects for people comfortable with risk, and comfort with risk correlates with being comfortable leaving. The person who joins you because the upside is exciting is reachable by the next company with a more exciting upside. Build for that rather than being wounded by it when it happens in year two.

## The uncomfortable version

Most employer branding advice for small companies is really advice about writing, and this is not a writing problem.

If you cannot name the decisions your next hire will own, cannot show them a number, and cannot think of a mistake you are willing to describe in public, then the ownership you are advertising does not exist yet, and a good candidate will work that out in the second interview. The artefacts are not a marketing exercise. They are a test of whether the offer is real, run on yourself, before a candidate runs it on you.

Do that test first. The careers page is the easy part afterwards.
