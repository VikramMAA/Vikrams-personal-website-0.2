Your positioning statement is on your homepage. Somebody probably ran a workshop to produce it.

So: what did it cost you in the last ninety days?

Not what it says. What it cost. Which deal did you turn away because of it, which service request did you decline, which channel did you stop running, which price did you hold when the client pushed.

If the honest answer is nothing, you do not have positioning. You have a tagline, and a tagline is a decoration on a business that still competes on price.

That is the easy half of the argument and most writing on this subject stops there, usually followed by an instruction to start saying no. Which is where it falls apart for an Indian services business, because that advice is written by people with a different balance sheet. So let me do the harder half, and then give you the version that survives contact with your bank account.

## A list is cheap, receipts are the test

I have argued before that a company should write down the profile it refuses to serve, in a piece about [growing a SaaS business in a small market](/blog/expansion-revenue-small-market-saas/). I stand by it, and I want to be harder on it than I was.

Writing the list is free. Everybody enjoys the workshop. The list gets printed and put on a wall.

The test arrives later, in a quarter where collections are slow and a prospect outside your stated focus turns up with a signed PO. That is the only moment the positioning is real or not, and nothing before it counts as evidence.

So the four receipts, from the last ninety days specifically, because ninety days is long enough to have faced a test and short enough that you remember it accurately. A declined deal. A refused scope request from an existing client. A channel or category you stopped. A discount you did not give.

One of the four is enough. Zero means the statement never met resistance, which in most markets means nobody is reading it.

## Why Indian services firms genuinely cannot just say no

Here is the structural thing the standard advice ignores, and it is not a confidence problem.

Recordent's Indian SME Receivables Report for 2026, drawing on roughly 1.1 lakh MSMEs, puts the average invoice payment cycle at 73 days against a 45 day regulatory benchmark. The same study finds 82.6% of invoices are written with credit periods of zero to 30 days, which means the gap is not generous terms, it is collection. And it estimates an average of around Rs 3.83 crore per SME sitting unpaid beyond 360 days.

Those are a commercial platform's own figures rather than independently audited ones, so hold them loosely. The direction matches everything I have seen.

Now read a positioning decision against that cash position.

A client you already serve asks whether you can also build their website. It is Rs 2 lakh, it arrives in six weeks, and your March salary run is not fully covered by what is collectible. Saying no to that is not a brave act of focus. It is a decision to be short of cash on a specific date, taken to protect a sentence on a web page.

**In India, positioning does not cost you pride. It costs you working capital.**

There is a legal lever, for completeness. Section 43B(h) of the Income Tax Act gives a buyer with a written agreement 45 days to pay an MSME supplier and 15 days without one, and the MSMED Act attaches compound interest beyond 45 days at three times the RBI bank rate. Almost nobody small invokes it against a client they want to keep. That is a real constraint, not a failure of nerve.

## So split the two lists

Here is the resolution, and it is the useful idea in this piece.

Stop trying to decide what you will do. Decide what you will advertise.

Those are different lists and almost every discussion of positioning collapses them. Take the website build. Do it, bill it, bank the Rs 2 lakh. But do not add web development to your services page, do not build a landing page for it, do not put it in the deck, do not let your sales person mention it on a first call, and do not write a blog post about it.

**You can take the work. You cannot market the work.**

The reason this holds is mechanical. What destroys a position is being found for the wrong reason, and being found happens through what you publish. A job you quietly do well for a client who already trusts you changes nothing about who arrives next month. A service listed on your homepage changes everything about it.

So the discipline moves to where it belongs, which is marketing, and stops being a prohibition on your sales team that they will ignore anyway when the quarter is tight.

## Three before and afters

These are composites of the pattern I see rather than any particular client's homepage. The lines change but the shape does not.

Before: full service digital marketing agency in Bengaluru.

After: we run paid acquisition for Indian D2C brands spending between Rs 5 lakh and Rs 50 lakh a month.

What the second one costs: every enquiry from a brand spending Rs 40,000, every business that wanted SEO, and every founder who wanted one vendor for everything. That is the bill. If you are not willing to pay it, keep the first line and stop calling it positioning.

Before: we build websites that grow your business.

After: Shopify builds for Indian brands that also sell on marketplaces.

Cost: every WordPress enquiry, every non-ecommerce project, every client with no marketplace presence. Gain: you can now say something specific about catalogue sync and duplicate listings that nobody else on the pitch mentions.

Before: technology partner for your digital transformation.

After: we migrate Indian insurers off legacy policy administration systems.

Cost: roughly 95% of the addressable market. Gain: when an insurer's CTO asks around, you are the only name that comes back twice.

Notice that every after sentence is longer and plainer than the before. Positioning is not a tightening exercise. It usually takes more words, because specificity costs words.

## The reason this matters more than it did in 2022

One genuinely new argument, and it is the one I would use on a sceptical founder.

When a buyer asks an assistant to recommend a vendor, something has to pick. A full service agency in Bengaluru gives a machine nothing to prefer it for, so the fallback is whoever has the most authority and the most mentions, which is not you.

A narrow claim is machine readable. "Paid acquisition for Indian D2C brands at Rs 5 to 50 lakh monthly spend" is a filter a model can match a query against. I have written about the mechanics of [getting quoted by ChatGPT](/blog/how-to-rank-in-chatgpt/), and the uncomfortable implication is that vagueness used to cost you conversion and now costs you consideration entirely.

## Where I come out

Positioning is not a sentence. It is a set of things you are no longer allowed to advertise, and the sentence is just the receipt.

Go and look at your last ninety days. If nothing was turned away, do not rewrite the homepage line, because a sharper sentence over the same behaviour fools nobody and will quietly revert within two quarters. Change what you publish first and let the sentence catch up.
