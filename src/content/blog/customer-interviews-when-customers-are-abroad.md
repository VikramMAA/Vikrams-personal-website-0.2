A founder in Munich selling to German mid-market companies learns things by accident.

A supplier grumbles about a customer at a trade breakfast. Somebody's brother-in-law works in procurement at the account they have been chasing for a year. Their new hire spent three years inside a buyer and knows exactly which internal document kills a purchase. None of that was research. It arrived.

You get none of it. Not less of it. None.

That is the actual export disadvantage for a company based here, and it is not a budget problem. Everything you know about your market has to have been deliberately requested by somebody, which makes research a standing operation rather than a project you run before a launch.

One boundary first, because it saves you from doing the wrong exercise. If your question is why a specific deal was won or lost, that is win/loss work, it has a method, and the method matters more than the questions. I set it out in [why did we lose that deal, and who should be asking](/blog/win-loss-analysis-for-small-business/), including why the person who owned the deal cannot be the person who asks. That post also says the thing worth repeating here: win/loss analysis answers questions about competitive evaluation and is structurally blind to demand, because you can only interview the people who got as far as a proposal.

This is about the other question. Not why that deal went that way, but what is actually true in a market you cannot observe.

## The three things you are replacing

Ambient contact gives a local competitor three specific assets, and it is worth being precise about them rather than talking vaguely about market understanding.

**The vocabulary.** The words buyers use for the problem, which are frequently not the words used for the category. This decides your website copy and your search terms, and the demand side of it is checkable from your desk in about an hour per market, which I went through in [international SEO for Estonian companies](/blog/international-seo-for-estonian-companies/).

**The buying process.** Who signs, in what order, and what internal document has to exist before anybody can say yes. You cannot infer this from a company's website and it is the single biggest cause of deals that stall at the end for no visible reason.

**The real list of alternatives.** Who they actually compared you against. For an exporter this is routinely a local incumbent you have never heard of, or doing nothing for another year, rather than the competitor on your pitch deck.

Those three are learnable by asking. Opinions about your product are not what you are after and are the least reliable thing anybody will tell you.

## Who to talk to when you have eleven customers

Start by accepting that you are not sampling. With a dozen customers across four countries you are collecting cases, and the honest standard is whether a conversation changed what you believe, not whether it was statistically valid. Anybody who tells you that you need thirty interviews is describing a project you will never run.

Three groups, in increasing order of difficulty.

**Your own recent customers**, recent enough to remember the alternatives. These calls are pleasant, the acceptance rate is high, and the founder asking personally gets accepted far more often than a researcher does, because the request is flattering rather than procedural.

**People who evaluated you and went elsewhere.** Harder, and the asking mechanics are the whole game, which is why I would send you to the win/loss post rather than improvise them here.

**People in your target market who have never heard of you.** Almost nobody tries this, and it is the only group that can tell you anything about demand. The ask that works is small and specific: a second-degree introduction on LinkedIn, fifteen minutes, an explicit statement that you are not selling anything, and one named question you want their view on. Then afterwards, send them something genuinely useful. That is how the second conversation happens.

## The language problem, which nobody warns you about

Run the interview in the buyer's working language if you possibly can, and if you cannot, understand precisely what you are losing.

You are not losing information. You are losing the hesitations, and the hesitations carry the signal.

"We could probably make that work" and "yes" are different answers. In a conversation held in a language that is second for both of you, both parties are quietly performing fluency, neither pauses where they would naturally pause, and the sentence comes out flat. You will write "yes" in your notes and it was not a yes.

Three honest options. A colleague who is native to that market runs the conversation while you listen, which is the best of them. Or you run it yourself and discount what you hear accordingly. Or you pay an interviewer, which is expensive and produces worse questions, because they do not know which answer would change your mind.

Two rules regardless. Never do it over email, because an email answer is a considered position statement and you want the unconsidered version. And use video rather than voice, because you need to see the pause.

## On recording it, briefly, because Europe is different

If you have an AI notetaker on the call, it is a recording device.

I covered the US state-by-state picture in [how to run a discovery call](/blog/how-to-run-a-discovery-call/). Europe is not the same shape, and one example is enough to make the point. Germany's criminal code, [in the official English translation](https://www.gesetze-im-internet.de/englisch_stgb/englisch_stgb.html), says at Section 201 that whoever "without being authorised to do so... makes an audio recording of the privately spoken words of another" incurs "imprisonment for a term not exceeding three years or a fine". Subsection 4 adds that the attempt is punishable. It is prosecuted on complaint rather than automatically.

I am not a lawyer and whether a given business call falls inside that provision is a question for one. The practical instruction does not depend on the answer: ask, in one sentence, every single time, and if the person hesitates at all, turn it off and take notes. Nobody has ever been offended by being asked.

## Six questions you can copy

Every one of them is about the last time something happened. None of them is about what somebody would do, because what people did is evidence and what they would do is imagination.

1. Walk me through the last time you bought something in this category. Where did it actually start?
2. Who else had to agree, and what did you have to send them?
3. What were you using before, or what were you doing instead?
4. What nearly stopped it?
5. What did you expect to be difficult that turned out not to be?
6. If this had not gone ahead, what would have happened instead?

The sixth one is the most valuable and the most skipped. The answer is usually nothing, or we would have carried on as we were, and that tells you who you are really competing with.

And one prohibition: never ask a question beginning "would you". The answer is always polite and always wrong.

## Before lunch

Two a month, forever, is the cadence. Not ten in a fortnight followed by nothing for a year, which is what everybody actually does and why nobody has notes from 2025.

So this is a Friday job rather than a quarterly one. Send three requests today: one recent customer, one person who went elsewhere, and one person in your target market who has never heard of you. Fifteen minutes each, no pitch, and one sentence saying why you are asking them specifically.

Two will say yes. That is enough to start, and the third will probably reply in January.
