Your proposal will be read by somebody who was not on the call.

They did not hear the conversation, they have no relationship with you, and they are reading it in a meeting you are not in, next to two others. That one fact determines almost everything about how it should be written, and almost no proposal is written as though it were true.

Which is why the documents that win do not read like brochures. They read like transcripts: a record of what the buyer said, in the buyer's own words, with a price attached.

So the structure is their situation first and in their language, then the specific outcome, then what is in and explicitly what is out, then one price, then a next step with a date on it. The part that does the most work is the sentence saying what you will not do.

## What the data says, and what it does not

There is real data here and it is worth reading carefully, because the popular summary of it is wrong.

Proposify's 2026 report covers 742,000 proposals sent through their platform. Winning proposals averaged **11 pages**. Losing proposals averaged **13**.

Two pages. That is the entire difference.

So when somebody tells you shorter proposals win, the evidence for a strong version of that claim is not there. What the numbers suggest is that length is close to irrelevant, which is more useful than a page target, because it means you can stop optimizing the wrong variable.

The report also says winning proposals were viewed about twelve times against eight, and read for twenty five minutes against sixteen. You will see this presented as advice. It is not advice, it is reverse causation: proposals get read for longer because the buyer is interested, not the other way round. Writing a longer document to manufacture reading time would be a misreading of your own dashboard.

One caveat on all of it. This is a sample of companies that use proposal software, so it skews toward firms with a repeatable sales process. And if you go looking for a benchmark win rate you will find 20%, 34%, 43% and 48% quoted in different places, which tells you the definitions are not shared. Use your own history.

## Their words, first and verbatim

Open with their situation, before anything about you.

Not a paraphrase and not your category for it. The actual phrasing they used on the call. If the operations director said "we're losing two days a week reconciling this by hand", that sentence goes in the document, in quotes if you like.

Two things happen. The person who was on the call recognizes themselves, which is the fastest trust available. And the person who was not on the call learns what the problem is from a colleague's mouth rather than from a vendor's, which is the only version they will believe.

This is also where the discovery call pays for itself. I wrote about [what to ask on that call](/blog/how-to-run-a-discovery-call/), including that a buying group is usually having an internal argument rather than making a decision. The proposal is the document that has to survive that argument when you are not in the room, and it can only do that if it is written in the vocabulary the room already uses.

If you cannot write this section in their words, you did not run the call properly, and the right move is to go back and ask rather than to write around the gap.

## The line that does the most work

Somewhere in the middle, state what is not included.

Not as a legal exclusion in nine-point type. As a plain sentence: "This does not include X, and if you need that we should talk about it separately."

I have watched this single element change more outcomes than any other, for a reason that seems backwards. A document that only lists what you will do reads like an unbounded promise, and unbounded promises make experienced buyers nervous, because they have been burned by exactly that and they know what the change order conversation looks like.

A document with a stated edge reads like it was written by somebody who has done this before. It also protects the engagement, which is the other half: the thing you excluded in writing in week zero is the thing you would otherwise be arguing about in month three.

## One price, not three

Put one number in, for the scope you described.

I argued the case against invented tiers at length when writing about [whether to publish pricing at all](/blog/should-i-put-pricing-on-my-website/), so briefly: buyers of bespoke work read Bronze, Silver and Gold as a device, and three options invite a conversation about which option rather than whether to proceed.

If the scope genuinely has a decision in it, give one price and one named addition, priced separately. That is a choice about the work. Three packages is a choice about how much to spend on you, which is a worse question to put in front of a committee.

## The credentials section everybody insists on

You will be told the proposal needs your team, your process and your awards. Here is the compromise I would make.

Put one piece of evidence in the body, chosen for this buyer: a short account of the same problem solved for somebody recognizably like them, with a number in it. One.

Everything else, the team biographies, the methodology diagram, the client logo wall, goes at the end or in a separate attachment. It is not useless, it is just not persuasive to the person deciding, and putting it on page two signals that the document is about you.

The test: if a reader stopped after page three, would they have seen their own problem, the outcome and the price? If what they would have seen is your founding story, the order is wrong.

## A next step with a date

End with one action, one owner and one date. "If this looks right, sign below and we start on the 12th."

Vague endings produce vague outcomes. "Let us know your thoughts" hands the document to a committee with no deadline, which is how proposals enter the state nobody calls a loss and everybody recognizes.

Make signing mechanically easy while you are at it. Proposify's data puts proposals with e-signature at a materially higher close rate and much faster, which is the one finding in their report I would act on directly, because unlike reading time it has no obvious reverse-causation problem: removing a printer from somebody's path plausibly speeds them up.

## Before lunch

An hour, on the last proposal you sent.

Read the first page and count the sentences that are about the buyer rather than about you. If it is fewer than half, you have found the thing to change.

Then check whether a single sentence anywhere says what is excluded. If not, write it now, for the next one.

Then look at the ending. One action, one owner, one date, or something softer? Rewrite it as a sentence with a date in it, and use that version from now on.
