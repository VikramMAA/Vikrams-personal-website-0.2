Almost everything you learned selling into Germany and the Nordics transfers to the UK. The language is easier, the market is large, and British buyers are comfortable with European suppliers.

One assumption does not transfer, and it is the one you do not know you are making: that crossing the border changes no paperwork.

Inside the single market that is roughly true. The UK is the nearest market where it is not, and the most expensive version of it is a VAT threshold that almost every guide quotes at you and that does not apply to your company.

So here is the useful version. Check your VAT position before your first invoice rather than after it, know that the data transfer question got easier rather than harder in December 2025, and in most cases do not bother setting up a UK entity.

## The threshold you have read about is not yours

The number in circulation is the UK registration threshold of £90,000 of taxable turnover. It is real and it is for businesses established in the UK.

[HMRC's own guidance on who should register](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/vat-notice-7001-should-i-be-registered-for-vat/vat-notice-7001-should-i-be-registered-for-vat) is blunt about what applies to everybody else. For a non-established taxable person, which is what your Estonian company is, the threshold does not apply at all. If you make taxable supplies in the UK of any value, you have to register. The deadline is 30 days from the first taxable supply or from the point you expected to make one, the effective date runs from whichever of those came first, and HMRC can register you retrospectively from the date the liability arose.

Now the part that saves most B2B companies, and it is the reason you have probably never heard about any of this.

For business-to-business services the place of supply is where the customer belongs, and the UK customer accounts for the VAT themselves under the reverse charge. So an Estonian company selling software or services to a UK business usually makes no UK taxable supply and has no UK registration duty. That is why your Finnish and German experience felt like it transferred.

Two things to watch, though, and one of them is genuinely new.

HMRC has published a [reform of the business-to-business reverse charge for services from overseas](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/reverse-charge-on-supplies-received-from-abroad/reform-of-the-business-to-business-reverse-charge-for-supplies-of-services-from-overseas). The direction is that the reverse charge would apply only where the UK purchaser is VAT registered, and where the purchaser is not registered the overseas supplier would have to charge and account for UK VAT. If a meaningful share of your UK pipeline is small businesses under the registration threshold, that is your problem rather than theirs. I could not confirm a commencement date from the published material, so treat it as a thing to check with an accountant before it bites rather than as a settled rule.

And if you sell to UK consumers rather than businesses, digital services included, the place of supply is the UK and you are registering from the first sale. No threshold, no grace period.

None of this is tax advice and it turns on exactly what you supply, which is the one question a generic article cannot answer for you. Spend an hour with somebody who does UK VAT for a living before your first invoice, not after your first thirty.

Goods are a larger and different problem, with customs declarations, commodity codes and import VAT, and I am not going to compress it into a paragraph here.

## The data question got easier, which nobody told you

If your UK advice is from 2024, it is wrong in your favour.

The European Commission renewed the UK adequacy decisions on 19 December 2025, and they now run to 27 December 2031, with a review of how they are working after about four years. The GDPR one is Decision (EU) 2025/2574. The renewal followed a technical extension while the Commission assessed the UK's Data (Use and Access) Act 2025.

The practical consequence for you is specific. Personal data moving from your Estonian company to a UK customer or a UK processor is an adequate transfer, so you do not need standard contractual clauses or a transfer impact assessment for that flow. The paragraph in your DPA that you copied from a template in 2023 and that mentions SCCs for UK transfers is now wrong, and a UK procurement team may well notice.

Two honest caveats. Adequacy is reviewable and the decision explicitly contemplates amendment or suspension if the UK diverges, so this is a current state rather than a permanent one. And it covers EU-to-UK flows: data coming back the other way sits on the UK's own adequacy regulations, which is a separate instrument.

Worth updating the document rather than just knowing it. I argued separately that [EU data residency is a sellable position rather than a compliance chore](/blog/gdpr-as-competitive-advantage/), and the same applies here in reverse: being the supplier who can explain the current basis in one sentence is a small, cheap credibility win.

## You probably do not need a UK entity

I have argued that a US entity is worth the trouble for European companies selling into America, and [why founders resist it and it works anyway](/blog/selling-saas-to-us-customers-from-europe/).

The UK is the market where I would say the opposite, and for reasons rather than out of balance. The language is shared, so nothing in your pitch needs translating. Adequacy means the data question has a clean answer. And British procurement is habituated to European suppliers in a way American procurement is not, so an Estonian OÜ on a contract raises fewer questions than a European entity does in Ohio.

What a UK buyer does want is smaller and cheaper than an entity.

An invoice in pounds, or at least a price in pounds somewhere on the page, because a prospect who has to convert is doing work you could have done.

Somebody answering during UK working hours, which is one hour behind you and therefore trivial.

A UK reference, named. This is the one that actually blocks deals, and it is why the first UK customer is worth taking at a bad price.

And a phone number that reaches a person. Not a form.

## What a UK buyer checks that a German one does not

One structural thing to unlearn from the market series so far.

The EU public procurement framework does not apply in the UK, so the tender logic I went through for [EU public sector buyers](/blog/selling-to-eu-public-sector-tenders/) does not carry across. Thresholds, notices and the standard forms are all different, and a UK public body is working to its own regime. If public sector is your target there, that is a separate piece of homework rather than an adaptation of what you already know.

In the private sector the difference is softer and mostly about evidence. German buyers, in my experience, ask for documents. British buyers ask for references and for who else like them is using it, and they ask earlier in the conversation. Which means your case studies matter more in the UK than your certifications do, and the reverse is true in Germany.

## Before lunch

An hour, and the first item is the one with a deadline attached.

Write down exactly what you sell to UK customers, business or consumer, service or goods, and send that description to an accountant who does UK VAT, with the question: do I have a registration duty, and from when?

Then open your DPA and your security page, find the clause about UK transfers, and replace the SCC language with the current adequacy basis.

Then put a pound price on the page, and go and find the one UK reference you are going to need for every deal after the first.
