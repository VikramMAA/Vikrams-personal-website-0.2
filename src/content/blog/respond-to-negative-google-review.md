The reply is the easy part. It is also the only part anybody writes about.

I gave the four-line version of it [when writing about getting reviews in the first place](/blog/how-to-get-more-google-reviews/), and the single thing worth repeating here is that the reply is written for the next prospect rather than for the reviewer. Once you accept that, the wording mostly answers itself.

What nobody tells you is the rest of it. Which reviews are worth answering at all, which ones Google will actually take down and on what grounds, and what to do when the third person in a row says the same thing.

Start with removal, because that is where the wasted effort goes.

## Google will not remove a review for being wrong

This is the misunderstanding that costs owners the most time. There is no policy ground for a review being inaccurate, unfair, or written by somebody who misremembered.

I went through [Google's prohibited and restricted content policy](https://support.google.com/contributionpolicy/answer/7400114) category by category to check, and nothing in it covers accuracy. What it does say, explicitly, is that the policy permits "content that describes negative experiences in a respectful manner". A truthful bad review is working as designed, and so is a false one that is politely worded.

So stop writing appeals that argue the facts. They are being read against a policy that does not ask about facts.

What the policy does cover is behavior, and five of those grounds come up constantly and almost never get cited properly.

**Off-topic.** The rule is that you should "only post content that is based on your experience or questions about experiences at a specific location". A review from somebody who was never a customer, or about a different branch, or about your industry in general, fails that test.

**Personal rants.** Under the same heading: "we don't allow content which contains general, political, or social commentary or personal rants". The political one is more common than you would think.

**Unsubstantiated allegations.** This is the ground nobody knows and it is the most useful one. The offensive content rules prohibit "unsubstantiated allegations of unethical behavior or criminal wrongdoing". A review that calls you a scam or accuses you of theft, with nothing behind it, is flaggable on exactly that wording.

**Personal information.** "Do not distribute or post personal information without consent." A review that names your technician and describes them is covered.

**Conflict of interest.** Content based on a conflict of interest is prohibited, and Google's wording extends to current or former employment and to contractual, consulting, professional or personal affiliations. The ex-employee and the competitor both sit here.

When you flag, cite the ground in its own language and point at the specific sentence that breaches it. One ground, one quote. An appeal that says "this is unfair and untrue" is asking the wrong question of the wrong policy.

And if a review clears all five and is simply bad, it is staying up. The remaining work is the reply, and the reply is enough.

## Not every review gets the same reply

Triage first, because the right response differs by what a reader will conclude.

**The fair complaint.** Highest value in the whole exercise. Reply fully, acknowledge the specific thing, and say what changed. A prospect reading this learns more about you than from any five-star review.

**The one with a wrong fact.** Reply briefly and correct exactly one thing, without adjectives. "We don't charge a call-out fee, so I think there may be a mix-up, and I'd like to check." One correction reads as confident. Three reads as a dispute, and nobody arbitrates a dispute in your favor.

**The non-customer.** Flag it as off-topic, then reply in one line stating you cannot find them in your records and inviting them to contact you. That line is for the reader, who otherwise assumes the complaint is real.

**The abusive one.** Flag it and do not engage. Any reply you write will be read next to the abuse and will inherit its tone.

**The one-liner with no content.** A bare two stars and no words. Reply shortest of all, or not at all. There is nothing to acknowledge, and a long reply to an empty review looks anxious.

One timing note: within a day or two is fine, and inside the first hour usually is not, because the reply you write angry is the one that gets screenshotted.

## The sentence that makes a reply credible

"We have taken this on board and are reviewing our processes" is the sound of nothing happening, and readers know it.

The version that works names the change. New policy, different supplier, extra person on Saturdays, a step added to the checklist. Which means the reply is only as good as whether you actually changed something, and that is the real work hiding inside a reputation question.

Which is also the argument for treating a bad review as research you did not have to pay for. Somebody has told you, in public and for free, where your process breaks. Most businesses spend the energy on the wording and none on the thing the wording is about.

If you cannot name a change, do not invent one. Acknowledge, apologize once, and offer the direct route to a named person. An honest reply with no promise beats a promise nobody keeps.

## When three people say the same thing

Here is the point at which replying becomes the wrong intervention entirely.

Read your last twenty reviews and count the complaints by cause rather than by star rating. If three of them mention the same twenty minutes of your process, you do not have a review problem. You have one operational fault that is being reported by the only people who have a public channel for it.

At that point the reply is damage control on a thing you could just fix, and the fix removes the whole stream of future reviews rather than the one in front of you.

This is the check I would run quarterly, and it takes fifteen minutes. Causes, not scores.

## A profile with no bad reviews reads as managed

The counterintuitive part, and it is worth internalizing before you go hunting for removals.

A buyer comparing three local businesses is not looking for perfection, because they do not believe in it. They sort the reviews lowest first, read the worst one, and then read your answer. That is the whole evaluation.

A profile with forty-eight reviews averaging 4.9 and nothing below four stars does not read as excellent. It reads as curated, which is a worse impression than one honest complaint handled well. And the tactics that produce that profile are the ones I covered separately, including that filtering who you ask based on how they feel is suppression under the current FTC rules rather than good practice.

So a two-star review with a calm, specific, change-naming reply underneath it is an asset. It is the only place a stranger gets to see how you behave when something goes wrong, and that is what they came to find out.

## Before lunch

Thirty minutes.

Open your worst three reviews. Check each against the five grounds above and flag only the ones that genuinely breach one, citing the wording.

Then write replies to the others, one correction maximum, one named change, one route to a real person.

Then read your last twenty reviews and tally the causes. If one cause appears three times, close the laptop and go and fix that instead.
