Your company is completely legitimate. Your website is doing a very good impression of a shell company.

That is an uncomfortable sentence and it is not an accusation. It is a description of a structural problem, because the signals that make a remote company efficient are the same signals that make a fraudulent one cheap.

No office, because you do not need one. No staff photos, because there are two of you in different countries. A registered address that belongs to a service provider, because that is how the system is designed to work. Payments through a fintech rather than a high street bank.

Every one of those is normal, sensible and above board. Every one of them is also on the mental checklist a cautious German procurement officer runs when deciding whether you exist.

You are not going to fix that by being more legitimate. You are already legitimate. You fix it by making the legitimacy visible, and most of it is an afternoon of work on pages you already have.

## The scale, because it helps to know

Something like 130,000 to 135,000 people from 180-odd countries hold Estonian e-Residency, and they collectively own more than 39,000 Estonian companies. A record 5,556 new businesses were registered through the programme in 2025, up around 15% on the year before, and those companies paid close to €125 million in direct tax in Estonia.

This is not a fringe arrangement and a well informed buyer knows that. The suspicion you encounter is almost never about the structure. It is about your specific website, which is telling them nothing.

One thing worth knowing from the other direction: audits on economic activity, transparency and tax compliance have tightened noticeably. It is no longer enough to open an OÜ and leave it there. The programme now expects a real business model and measurable activity.

Which is convenient, because the evidence that reassures a buyer is largely the same evidence that demonstrates genuine activity. You are building one thing for two audiences.

## What a cautious buyer actually checks

In roughly this order, in about ninety seconds.

Is there a named human anywhere on this site. Does that human exist elsewhere on the internet, with a history that predates the company. Is there a registry number and a VAT number. Where is the address and does it belong to twelve hundred other companies. Has anybody else bought from them and said so publicly. What happens if this goes wrong.

If four of those six come back empty, they do not accuse you of anything. They just quietly choose the other supplier and you never learn why.

## The page by page fix

**Put names and faces on the about page.** Real photographs, full names, and what each person actually did before this. A founder whose LinkedIn goes back eight years is the single strongest signal available to you, and it costs nothing because it already exists. Link to it.

The version that fails is a page describing "our team of experts" with no person named. That reads worse than an empty page.

**Be honest about where you are.** Do not imply an office you do not have. Do not list "offices" in three cities because your contractors live there.

The honest version converts better anyway. Something like: "We are a registered Estonian company. Our team works remotely from Tallinn, Lisbon and Kraków. Our registered address is provided by our service provider, which is standard for Estonian companies." That sentence takes the suspicion off the table by naming it first, and a buyer who reads it stops looking for what you are hiding.

**Show the registry number and the VAT number in the footer.** Free, expected across the EU, and instantly checkable. A buyer who can verify you in the Estonian business register in fifteen seconds has stopped worrying. Confirm your own VAT registration position with your accountant, since it depends on your turnover and where you sell.

**Publish proof of work.** Named clients with a sentence on what you did and what changed. If you cannot name them, describe the engagement concretely: the sector, the size, the problem, the outcome, the year. A described engagement with real specifics beats a logo wall of companies you did trivial work for.

Testimonials need a full name, a company and ideally a photograph. "Great service, M.K." is worse than no testimonial at all, because it tells the reader you could not get permission.

**State your terms plainly.** Governing law, payment terms, what happens if the work is late, how to cancel. Most small remote companies hide this because it feels like admitting things go wrong. Buyers read a clear terms page as evidence of somebody who has done this before.

**Give a phone number that a person answers.** Even if it is a mobile. Especially if it is a mobile.

## Payments, which is where the doubt often lands

Estonian companies are not required to hold an Estonian bank account. Any EU or EEA account is fine, and plenty of legitimate companies run on fintech accounts.

What matters commercially is what your invoice looks like to their finance team. An EU IBAN, an invoice with your registry and VAT numbers, and payment terms that match what you promised. If your invoices go out from a personal-looking account with no company details, that undoes everything the website achieved.

For anything sold to consumers rather than businesses, the checkout carries its own trust burden, and I went through [what Estonian buyers expect there](/blog/estonian-ecommerce-payment-methods/) separately.

## What actively makes it worse

Stock photographs of a team that does not exist. People recognise these and the recognition is fatal.

Claiming offices you do not have. One buyer who checks is enough.

A vague "founded in 2019" when the register says 2024. Small inconsistencies do disproportionate damage, because the buyer stops evaluating your service and starts auditing your claims.

Language that is bigger than the company. "Trusted by industry leaders worldwide" from a two person firm reads as a lie even when the two people are excellent. Smaller and specific beats larger and vague, which is the same argument I made about [how to write for Nordic buyers](/blog/how-to-sell-to-nordic-customers-from-estonia/) and it applies to most of Europe.

## Before lunch

Four things, about ninety minutes.

Open your own site in a private window and try to find a full human name in under fifteen seconds. If you cannot, that is the first fix.

Add your registry number and VAT number to the footer today.

Write the honest location paragraph, the one that says remote and says where, and put it on the about page. Read it back and check it contains nothing you would not say out loud on a call.

Then pick one past project and write it up with a sector, a number and a year, even without the client's name.

None of this is marketing in the interesting sense. It is removing reasons to say no, which is almost always cheaper than generating more reasons to say yes, and it matters more the further your buyer is from you.
