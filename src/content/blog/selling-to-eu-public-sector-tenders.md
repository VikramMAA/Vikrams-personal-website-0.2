Most of the EU public money available to an Estonian company is not advertised on TED.

If Tenders Electronic Daily is the only place you look, you are watching the slice of the market where every competitor in Europe is also looking, and ignoring the larger and quieter part that never has to be published there at all.

That is the first thing to fix, and it follows from a set of numbers almost nobody bidding for the first time has read.

The rest of it is mechanical rather than clever. Know which threshold your contract sits under, because it decides where the notice appears. Chase frameworks rather than individual contracts, because the framework is the gate. Assemble your qualification paperwork once and reuse it. And accept that the mandatory criteria will disqualify you on a technicality regardless of how good your offer is, which is a solvable problem if you look at it before you bid rather than after.

## The numbers that decide where a contract appears

These are revised every two years and the current set runs to the end of 2027, under Commission Delegated Regulation (EU) 2025/2152. [The Commission publishes them directly](https://single-market-economy.ec.europa.eu/single-market/public-procurement/rules-implementation/thresholds_en), and they are worth having in front of you.

For contracts under the main procurement directive, excluding VAT:

**Central government authorities.** Services, design contests and supplies at **EUR 140,000**. Subsidised services at **EUR 216,000**. Works at **EUR 5,404,000**. Social and other specific services listed in Annex XIV at **EUR 750,000**.

**Sub-central authorities**, meaning municipalities, regional bodies and most of the organisations you will actually deal with. Services, design contests and supplies at **EUR 216,000**. Works at the same **EUR 5,404,000**. Annex XIV services again at **EUR 750,000**.

Above those figures, the contract must be advertised EU-wide. Below them, national rules apply, and the notice lives on the member state's own portal.

**So the sub-threshold market is both larger in number and advertised only where locals look.**

For an Estonian company that means two habits, not one. Watch TED for the big contracts. Watch the national registers of the countries you actually want to work in for everything else, starting with Estonia's own public procurement register, where the contracts you have the best chance of winning are the ones nobody outside the country is reading about.

One detail worth noticing: this cycle the thresholds moved slightly down, from EUR 143,000 and EUR 221,000 and EUR 5,538,000. Lower thresholds mean marginally more contracts have to be published Europe-wide, which is to your advantage if you are outside the buyer's country.

## Frameworks are the actual gate

This is the structural thing that first-time bidders miss, and getting it wrong wastes a year.

A great deal of public buying does not happen through individual tenders. It happens through framework agreements: a body runs one procurement, admits a set of suppliers for two to four years, and then buys repeatedly from inside that set through call-offs that are never publicly advertised again.

Which means if you only bid on individual notices, you are competing for the contracts that fall outside the frameworks, while the steady work goes to suppliers who won a competition you did not enter, possibly three years ago.

Find out which frameworks cover your category and when they are next re-tendered. Those dates are knowable and they are the most valuable thing in your pipeline, because a framework award is worth years of call-offs and the competition for it happens once.

If a framework in your category was awarded last year, that is not bad luck, it is a planning input. You now know the quarter you need to be ready in.

## Assemble the paperwork once

Most of the effort in a first bid is not the offer, it is proving you are allowed to bid at all, and almost all of that is reusable.

The European Single Procurement Document exists for this. It is a standard self-declaration about your exclusion grounds and your qualification, accepted across member states, and the point of it is that you fill it in properly once and adapt it rather than starting fresh.

Behind it, keep a folder that is always current: tax clearance, the company registration extract, your insurance certificates with the cover levels stated, audited accounts, and a reference list with contract values and dates against each. Refresh it quarterly whether or not you are bidding, because the one you assemble in bid week will be the weakest version of it you ever submit.

For an Estonian company this is less work than for almost anybody else. Digital ID, e-signature and e-invoicing are already how you operate, and the administrative apparatus that other bidders find painful is the part you can do in an afternoon.

## The criteria that disqualify you regardless of merit

Here is where good offers die, and it has nothing to do with quality.

Mandatory requirements are pass or fail. Minimum annual turnover, often expressed as a multiple of the contract value. Professional insurance at a stated level. Prior contracts of similar scale, which is the one that locks out newcomers, because you cannot have delivered a EUR 400,000 contract until somebody lets you deliver one.

Miss one and the evaluators do not read the rest. They are not allowed to.

Read those criteria before anything else, and if you fail one, the answer is usually not to bid anyway and hope. There are two legitimate routes out, and both are built into the rules.

You can rely on the capacity of another entity, which the directive expressly permits, so a partner's turnover or technical references can support your bid provided the arrangement is real and documented.

Or you bid as a consortium, jointly with a firm that has the references you lack while you bring the thing they do not have. For a first contract in a new country this is usually the fastest honest route in, and your second bid is the one you make alone.

And use the clarification window. Every procedure has one, it is the only legitimate way to influence or clarify a specification, and most bidders ignore it entirely. If a requirement looks like it was written around a specific incumbent product, asking in writing whether an equivalent is acceptable sometimes changes the specification for everybody.

## One mindset correction

I have [argued elsewhere that a rigid process and a short deadline often mean a commercial deal is already decided](/blog/should-i-respond-to-rfps/). In public procurement that reasoning is wrong, and applying it will make you give up on winnable work.

Here the rigidity is the law. The buyer cannot have a friendly chat with you during a live procedure, the criteria cannot be changed to suit you after publication, and the scoring has to be defensible if somebody challenges it. A process that feels impersonal is a process working as designed.

So the instinct that wins commercial deals, which is to find the decision maker and build a relationship, is the instinct to suppress. What replaces it is earlier timing: engage with the buying organisation long before a procedure exists, through market consultations and supplier days, which are legitimate and are where the specification is actually shaped.

## Before lunch

An hour, and the first part is the one people skip.

Open your national procurement register and filter for your category, then do the same on TED. Compare the two lists and count. That ratio tells you how much of your market you have not been watching.

Then pick one framework agreement in your category and find its expiry date. Put it in the calendar with six months of lead time.

Then start the folder. Tax clearance, registration extract, insurance certificates, accounts, reference list with values and dates. You will reuse it for years and it is the cheapest competitive advantage available in this whole area.
