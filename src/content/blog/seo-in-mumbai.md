Draw a ten kilometre circle around your office on a map of Mumbai.

Look at what is inside it. A reasonable share is water. Another slice is somewhere nobody from your area would travel to on a weekday. And a chunk of the people who actually walk through your door live outside the circle entirely, fifteen kilometres up the line, because the train takes twenty five minutes and they do it twice a day anyway.

That circle is how most targeting is set up, and in this city it is simply the wrong shape.

Mumbai is a strip. Movement happens along axes, not outward in rings. Which means the distance that matters is not how far somebody is from you but whether they are on your line, and those two numbers disagree constantly.

## The consequence, stated plainly

Your catchment is a corridor, not a radius.

Somebody in Borivali and somebody in Churchgate are both on the Western side and both plausibly yours depending on what you sell. Somebody five kilometres across the harbour is nearer on the map and effectively unreachable.

A business that sets a radius has included a lot of people who will never come and excluded the ones who already do. And it will then conclude that search does not work in its category, when what failed was the geometry.

**In a linear city, a radius is not a conservative choice. It is a wrong one.**

The fix costs nothing and you already have the data. Take your last fifty customers and write down where each one travelled from. Not their billing address, where they came from. That list is your corridor, and it will not look like a circle.

## The head term is a decision to lose slowly

Here is the second thing, and it is where real money goes.

The phrase with Mumbai in it is contested by national portals, aggregators and companies with budgets that are not in your range. Real estate, finance and hospitality are the worst of it, and in those categories the first page is effectively bought.

So competing for the city is not ambitious, it is a slow loss. You will spend for two years and finish on page three behind a directory.

And it is not what people type anyway. Nobody searching seriously types a dentist in Mumbai. They type the locality, because the locality is the real unit of decision here. Andheri East and Andheri West are different markets with different buyers and different commutes, and treating them as one place is the same mistake as the radius in a different costume.

I went through the mechanics of [building locality pages properly](/blog/local-seo-for-real-estate-agents-india/) in the context of property, and the structure transfers to any service business. What changes in Mumbai is only how narrow you have to go.

## What actually differentiates in a crowded local result

When five competitors are all technically competent, the separators are unglamorous.

**Reviews with the locality in them.** A review that mentions Chembur does work that no amount of on-page text does, because it is somebody else saying where you are. You cannot write those. You can ask customers at the right moment, which is [the part most businesses never systematise](/blog/google-business-profile-settings-bengaluru/).

**Hours that are true, including the ones nobody updates.** In a city where people decide at nine at night whether to travel tomorrow, a wrong closing time is a lost customer and a one star review waiting to happen.

**Photographs of the entrance and the street.** Mumbai addresses are genuinely hard to find. A picture of your actual door, and one of the landmark opposite, converts better than anything you could write, and it reduces the calls asking where you are.

**Answering the phone.** Still the largest uncontrolled variable in every category I have looked at, and it has nothing to do with search.

None of that is clever. All of it is available this week, and in a competitive result it is what separates the business that gets the call from the four that were equally findable.

## Being honest about the remote part

I should be straight about my own position here, since this post will be read by people searching for somebody in Mumbai.

I do not have an Andheri office. I work with Mumbai businesses from elsewhere, and I am not going to pretend that is the same as being down the road.

What proximity genuinely buys, in my experience, is two things. Somebody who can be in the room when the room matters, which for most digital work is rarer than people expect. And somebody who already knows which corridor your buyer is on, which is real knowledge and is also the single thing you can hand over in one conversation with your own customer list in front of you.

What it does not buy is better execution on search, paid media or measurement. Those travel fine, and the firms that insist otherwise are usually selling the office rather than the work.

So if you want somebody local, the thing to test is whether they can tell you something about your corridor you did not already know. If they can, that is worth paying for. If the local pitch is mostly about meetings, you are buying a commute.

## The uncomfortable part

Most Mumbai businesses I have looked at are not losing on search because of competition. They are losing because they are competing for the wrong unit.

The category is brutal at the city level and surprisingly soft at the corridor level, which is where the buyers with intent actually are. A dental practice that owns the Chembur result does not need to rank in Mumbai. A B2B firm that owns a named micro market and two adjacent ones has more pipeline than one that is page four for the state.

Narrowing feels like giving up. It is the only move available that you can actually win, and in this city it is a geographic argument rather than a marketing preference.

## Where I come out

Stop drawing circles. Pull your last fifty customers, find your corridor, and build for that.

Then pick the two localities where you already have customers and go and own those results properly, with reviews that name the place, hours that are correct, and photographs somebody can navigate by.

Leave the city term alone until you have won something smaller. It is not going anywhere and you cannot afford it yet.
