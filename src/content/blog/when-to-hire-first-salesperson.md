You are not hiring a salesperson. You are trying to stop doing something you have come to dread.

That is a real feeling and I would not pretend otherwise. It is also a bad brief, because the part you dread is usually the part that has not been worked out yet, and handing an unsolved problem to an employee does not solve it. It just moves the failure somewhere you can observe it less clearly.

So the question is not when you can afford a salesperson. It is whether there is a sale to hand over, and that is checkable.

## Four things you should be able to answer

Not a feeling about readiness. Four specific answers.

**Can you predict the objection before the prospect finishes the sentence?** I made this point in passing [when writing about first hires in a thin market](/blog/first-marketing-hire-estonia/), and it is the best single test there is. For most B2B products you get there somewhere around thirty or forty real conversations.

**Which lead source closes, as opposed to which produces leads?** Different question, and most founders cannot answer it. Referrals might be a fifth of your pipeline and half your signatures. If you do not know, you will hand your new hire a list sorted by the wrong thing and then conclude they cannot sell.

**Can you write the sequence down?** First contact to signature, as a numbered list somebody else could follow. Not a diagram of a funnel. The actual steps, who does what, and what has to be true before each one. If it cannot be written, there is nothing to hand over, because what you have is in your head and it is not transferable by osmosis.

**Can you name the three reasons you lose, with rough frequencies?** Not "price". Three named reasons. Lost to incumbent inertia, lost on implementation effort, lost because the champion changed jobs. If you cannot do this, you do not have a sales process. You have a personality, and personalities do not come with a second copy.

Answer all four and you have something to delegate. Answer two and your hire is an outsourced discovery project at a salary.

## The ramp number you will be quoted does not apply to you

Somebody will tell you what ramp to expect, so here is the honest state of that figure.

The number in circulation comes from Bridge Group's 2026 research, reported as an average account executive ramp of about 6.2 months across 158 B2B companies and described as the longest in that study's history. SDR ramp is put nearer three months. Those come to me through secondary write-ups rather than the original report, so treat them as the shape rather than as your number.

More importantly, read what that figure is measuring. It is how long a rep takes to reach productivity at a company that already has a sales process to ramp into: a playbook, a qualified pipeline, a manager who has done the job.

Your first hire has none of that. So six months is a floor rather than an estimate, and the honest planning assumption is longer.

Which gives you the gate that actually decides this. **Can you fund twelve months of that person with nothing attributed to them?** If not, the hire is a bet you cannot hold, and bets you cannot hold get abandoned around month five, which is the most expensive possible outcome. You pay for the ramp and then quit before any of it pays back.

## The first hire is probably not a closer

Here is where I would push back on the standard advice, which is to hire an account executive.

Look at where your week actually goes. For most founders doing their own selling, the selling itself is maybe a fifth of the hours. The rest is research, list building, chasing a reply, rescheduling, assembling the proposal, updating the CRM, writing the follow-up, finding the case study to attach.

So consider hiring that instead. Somebody capable who takes all of it, and whose job is to make sure that when you sit down, there is a qualified conversation waiting and a proposal half-built.

Done properly that takes your selling from a fifth of your week to well over half. You have roughly tripled your own selling capacity without transferring any of the judgment you have not yet codified. It costs less, it fails more cheaply, and crucially it buys you the months in which you can answer the four questions above.

Then hire the closer, into a process that exists, with somebody already doing the support work they would otherwise have to do themselves.

## You cannot incentivise your way out of not knowing what works

On commission, two things, and the second one matters more.

Pure commission for a first hire selects for the wrong person. Think about who accepts it: somebody willing to work unpaid on an unproven product with no track record of anybody succeeding there. That is usually either desperation or somebody carrying your product as one of five in a bag, and the second one behaves exactly as you would expect, which I went through [about resellers](/blog/reseller-partner-channel-small-market/).

The deeper problem is what commission is for. It is a mechanism for motivating behavior you already know produces revenue. Before you know that, the plan pays for the wrong activity, and a competent rep will optimize hard toward whatever you chose to measure. Pay per meeting booked and you will get meetings. Pay per deal closed with no floor on discount and you will find out what your price really is.

So: a real base, a modest variable, and a plan you expect to rewrite in six months when you understand what good looks like. Say that out loud at the offer stage, because a rep who cannot live with an evolving plan is telling you something useful.

## The handoff that nobody actually does

The transfer is where this falls apart, and the fix is uncomfortable rather than complicated.

Co-sell the first ten deals. The new person leads the call. You attend and you stay silent.

The silence is the hard part. You will know the better answer to the question that was just asked, and you will want to give it, and giving it tells the prospect who the real decision maker is, which means every deal after that one routes back to you.

Then debrief after each call on one question: what would you have said differently and why? That is the mechanism by which the thing in your head becomes the thing in theirs, and there is no shortcut version of it.

And plan for the possibility that it does not work. A first sales hire failing is common and is not a verdict on you or on them. The founders who handle it best are the ones who expected to be back in the seat within a year if it went that way, and who kept enough of their own relationships warm to make that survivable.

## Where I come out

Wait until you can answer the four questions, hire the support role before the closer, pay a real base, and stay on the calls long after it feels necessary.

One honest exception, because there is a real case against everything above. If you are genuinely bad at selling, or you dislike it enough that you avoid it, you are not a founder-led sales company. You are a company with no sales effort, and a mediocre rep beats that comfortably. In that situation hire early, accept that the first year is tuition, and be honest in the interview that the process does not exist yet.

What does not work is hiring to escape, while telling yourself and the candidate that there is a playbook.
