You found it this morning and you have been angry ever since. Your own sentences, your own structure, in some cases your own typos, sitting on somebody else's domain.

The anger is proportionate to the theft. The damage is a different number, and it is usually much smaller, which matters because it changes what is worth spending a week on.

There are three separate problems here and they get argued as one. What it does to your search rankings. What it does to a buyer who sees both sites. And what you can actually make happen about it.

The first one is the smallest, and I would deal with it first only to stop worrying about it.

## The search problem is smaller than you think

The fear is that Google will see two identical pages and penalise yours. That is not how it works, and Google has said so for a long time in its own words.

Google's post [demystifying the duplicate content penalty](https://developers.google.com/search/blog/2008/09/demystifying-duplicate-content-penalty) states plainly that there is no such thing, and the current SEO starter guide repeats that duplicate content is not something that causes a manual action. What Google does instead is cluster duplicate pages and pick one as canonical, choosing the version it judges most complete and useful.

Which is usually yours, because you have the earlier crawl date, the inbound links, the surrounding pages that make the topic coherent, and a domain with history.

The line that should settle your evening is in the starter guide's own framing: duplicate content is not a problem, but copying somebody else's content is a different story. The ranking risk sits with the copier, not with you. Google's older guidance says adjustments are made where duplication looks intended to manipulate rankings and deceive users, and that is a description of what they did rather than of what you did.

So check one thing and then stop. Search a distinctive sentence from the page in quotes. If your page comes up first, the search problem is already resolved and no action is needed.

## What a Google removal actually does, and what it does not

If you want to escalate, understand what you are buying, because most people misunderstand this completely.

A copyright removal request to Google removes the URL from Google Search. It does not remove the content from their website. The page stays up, their customers can still reach it, and anybody with the link still sees your words.

If you want the page gone, that is a conversation with their host, not with Google.

Three things worth knowing before you file anything. The request goes through Google's legal removal process rather than an ordinary content report, and Google is explicit that a report filed through a content policy form does not count as legal notice. You will be asked to state a good faith belief that the use is not authorised and to affirm, under penalty of perjury, that you are authorised to represent the copyright owner, which is not a box to tick casually. And requests get declined for insufficient detail about what is infringing, for the content not being findable at the URL given, for improper use of the process, and for fair use.

The copier can also file a counter-notice, after which the link may be reinstated.

Google's transparency reporting has put average processing for web form Search requests at around six hours, though that figure sits on a page several years old, so treat it as an indication rather than a promise.

## The faster route is usually the host

For most Indian businesses in this situation, the practical sequence is unglamorous and works more often than the legal one.

**Evidence first, before you contact anybody.** Capture your own page and theirs as dated archives, using the Wayback Machine or a timestamped PDF. Note the dates. If your page has been live for three years and theirs appeared last month, that comparison is the entire case and it takes ten minutes to document. Do this before you complain, because the moment they know, the page changes.

**Then the host.** Find who hosts the site and write to their abuse address, with the two URLs, the specific passages side by side, and your evidence of priority. Hosts deal with this routinely and have a direct lever that no amount of correspondence with the copier does.

**Then the registrar, if the host is unresponsive.** Less effective, occasionally the thing that moves it.

**Then a lawyer's notice, if the amount at stake justifies it.** A letter from an advocate costs real money and often produces compliance on its own, but ask yourself what you are buying before you spend it. I am not a lawyer and none of this is legal advice, which matters more here than in most of what I write.

The honest observation: a polite, specific email to the copier naming the passages and asking them to remove them resolves this more often than anybody expects. A surprising share of copied sites were built by an agency the owner hired, who padded the site from competitors without telling them. The owner is frequently embarrassed rather than defiant.

## The commercial problem, which is the one that actually costs you

Now the part worth your attention, because it is the one with money attached.

A buyer comparing two suppliers sees the same paragraphs on both sites. They will not know who wrote them first, because nobody checks crawl dates. What they conclude is that this is boilerplate, that both companies are interchangeable, and that the only remaining difference is price.

That is the real cost, and it is not a legal problem at all. The copying did not create it. The copying revealed that your pages were copyable, which means they contained nothing only you could have written.

So the structural fix is the same work as the remedy, and it is the only part of this that compounds.

Fill your important pages with things that cannot be lifted. Numbers from your own jobs. Photographs of your own work, your own premises, your own people. Named references and the specific town or industry they are in. The thing that went wrong on a project and what you did about it. A price, or an honest explanation of what moves it.

A competitor can copy all of that too, in the literal sense, and if they do it becomes verifiably false about them, which is a far better position for you than an argument about paragraphs.

## When to let it go

Sometimes, and more often than pride allows.

If your page still ranks first, if the copier is small and local and not competing for your customers, and if their site is visibly worse, the copy is doing them more harm than you. A prospect who finds both tends to pick the one that looks like the original, and originals look like originals.

Spending a fortnight and Rs 30,000 of a lawyer's time to remove four paragraphs from a site nobody visits is a decision made with your feelings rather than your calendar.

## Before lunch

Forty minutes, in this order.

Search a distinctive sentence from your page in quotes and confirm you rank above the copy. That alone will lower your blood pressure.

Then archive both pages with dates, before you contact anybody.

Then send one short, specific, unemotional email to the site owner naming the passages, and give it a week before you escalate to the host.

Then spend the rest of the hour adding one thing to your most important page that nobody could copy without lying.
