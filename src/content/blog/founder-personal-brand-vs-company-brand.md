Before a procurement team in Gurgaon approves anything with your company, somebody fairly junior runs two searches.

One is your company's name. The other is yours.

Those two searches return completely different kinds of thing, and the difference is the whole argument. The company search returns existence. The registry will confirm you were incorporated, the GST number will check out, the website loads. That is all it confirms.

Your name returns evidence of judgement. What you have said about this problem, what you have shipped, who has publicly vouched for you, whether you sounded like somebody who had done this before or somebody who had read about it.

Existence is cheap to establish and tells a buyer nothing about their actual risk. Judgement is expensive to fake and it is the only thing that answers the question they are really asking, which is whether this is going to go wrong and whether they will be blamed.

So founder-led visibility is not vanity in this market. It is supplying the only evidence that a registry cannot.

## Which makes the usual advice wrong in both directions

The two available positions on this are both lazy.

One says founders must build in public, as though visibility were a virtue in itself, which produces a lot of Indian founders posting daily about discipline and none of it answering a buyer's question.

The other says personal brands are ego, do the work and let it speak, which is advice that only functions if somebody already knows who you are. The work does not speak. It has no mouth.

The honest version is narrower. At the start, your name is doing a job your company name cannot yet do, because the company has no history and you have some. That is rational. It is also temporary, and almost nobody plans for the second part.

## What you are actually building, and why it will not sell

Here is the part worth sitting with.

Everything you build on your own name is an asset you cannot sell, cannot delegate, and cannot take a quarter away from. It does not appear on your balance sheet and it does not transfer to whoever runs the company after you.

I made the operational version of this argument yesterday, about [what stops when the owner takes two weeks off](/blog/what-happens-when-you-take-two-weeks-off/). This is the brand version of the same problem, and in India it is worse for one specific reason.

Your relationships live in your personal WhatsApp.

Not in a CRM, not in a shared inbox, in the chat history on a phone that belongs to you, under a number that is yours. Which means the relationship is not merely attached to your name, it is attached to your handset. I went through why that history is more fragile than people assume in [why your WhatsApp list is an asset you do not own](/blog/whatsapp-list-you-do-not-own/), and the two problems compound: the trust is in your name and the record of it is nowhere.

A buyer in Chennai who has dealt with you for three years has a relationship with a person and a phone number. If you step back, there is nothing for them to transfer their confidence to, because nothing else in the company ever earned any.

## The signals that it is time to start transferring

This is the question nobody asks at the right moment, which is well before it becomes urgent.

Four signals, and if you have two of them you are already late.

**Inbound arrives addressed to you for work you do not personally do.** Somebody emails you asking for a thing your team delivers, because you are the only name attached to it. Every one of those is a small tax you have chosen to keep paying.

**Clients escalate to you rather than to the person running their account.** Not because that person is bad. Because the client's confidence was never in them.

**Your name is in the proposal where the company's should be.** Check the last one you sent. Count the mentions of you against the mentions of the company, and count how many of yours could have been a named colleague instead.

**And the one that settles it: your team describes a decision as what you would have wanted, rather than as a rule.** That tells you the judgement never became company property, which is the same failure in a different department.

## How the transfer actually works, briefly

Not by going quiet. Going quiet just removes the only evidence a buyer had and replaces it with nothing.

It works by attaching other names to the things your name currently carries. Somebody else writes the post and you reply to it in public rather than writing it yourself. Somebody else takes the client call and you do not join. Somebody else's byline goes on the case study, and the client quote names them, not you.

And one that costs nothing and almost nobody does: when a client thanks you for something a colleague did, correct them by name, in writing, in the thread. That single habit moves more credit than any content plan, because the buyer updates their mental model of who is competent at your company.

On what to actually post while you are still the one posting, I set out the formats that work for Indian B2B in [what a B2B founder in India should post on LinkedIn](/blog/linkedin-content-strategy-b2b-india/), including why the company page is not the place to do it.

## Where I come out

The question in the title has an answer and it is yes, at the start, for most Indian B2B companies, and the people telling you otherwise are usually describing a market where company names carry history that yours does not have yet.

But the thing you are building is a dependency, and the right time to start dismantling it is while it still feels unnecessary. A founder brand that is still growing in year six is not a success, it is a company that never got around to becoming one.
