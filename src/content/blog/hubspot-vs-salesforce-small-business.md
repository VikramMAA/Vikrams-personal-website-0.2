Before anything else, answer this.

Name the person who will be sitting in the settings screen at three in the afternoon on an ordinary Tuesday next March, working out why the deal stage automation stopped firing.

A specific human being. Not "we'll figure that out" and not "the agency does it."

If you can't name them, nothing below this line matters, because you're about to buy a system that will be configured once, drift for four months and then be quietly worked around in a spreadsheet. I've watched that happen more often than I've watched a small company outgrow its CRM.

My stated basis, since a comparison without one is just a preference: this is drawn from published pricing at the time of writing and from watching small companies live with both. I have no partnership with either vendor and no referral arrangement. Prices in both products move, so check them yourself before you commit.

## What each one is genuinely good at

Both of these are good products. That's worth saying plainly, because most comparisons need one of them to be bad and neither is.

**Salesforce** will do whatever you need it to do. That is not marketing copy, it's an accurate description of a platform that has been extended for two decades. If your sales process has genuine complexity, multiple business units, an approval chain, territory rules, unusual objects, it will model that, and it will keep modelling it at a thousand people. Nobody gets fired for it and every senior hire you make will already know it.

**HubSpot** will be in use by Thursday. The interface is genuinely good, the marketing and sales sides actually share data instead of being bolted together, and a competent marketing person can build a workflow without a developer. For a company where sales and marketing are three people who also do other jobs, that matters more than extensibility does.

Those two strengths are aimed at different problems, which is why the feature-by-feature comparison you were about to read resolves nothing. They both do contacts, deals, pipelines, email, reporting. Of course they do.

## The price you were quoted is not the price

Here's where I'd push back on both, at the level of pricing structure rather than either company.

Published HubSpot pricing has four moving parts: core seats, marketing contacts, AI credits and a one-time onboarding fee. Starter sits around $20 per seat per month, which is genuinely cheap. Professional starts near $800 a month, includes three seats, and charges around $45 for each seat after that. Cross 2,000 marketing contacts and you buy more in blocks. Onboarding on the upper tiers is a mandatory few thousand dollars.

Salesforce publishes Starter Suite around $25 per user per month and Pro Suite around $100, with Enterprise well above that. The AI layer is sold separately under several models at once: roughly $2 per conversation, or flex credits at about $500 per 100,000, or per-user licensing from around $125.

Read those two paragraphs again and notice what they have in common. Seats are the smallest variable in both. The parts that actually move your invoice are contacts, credits and consumption, and none of them are knowable in advance.

**Consumption pricing on a core system means a small company cannot forecast a line item it cannot switch off.**

That isn't a complaint about either vendor, both are pricing the way the whole category now prices. It's a warning about what you're signing. Model your cost at three times your current contact count and at whatever AI usage you'd have if the features actually worked, because that is the invoice you'll be looking at in year two.

## The question that actually decides it

Back to the Tuesday afternoon.

Salesforce assumes an administrator exists. That assumption is baked into the product and it's reasonable, because at the size Salesforce was designed for, one does. A small company that buys it without one ends up in a predictable place: a consultant configured it during onboarding, the configuration no longer matches how the team sells, and changing anything requires paying the consultant to come back. The system becomes a cost you maintain rather than a tool you use.

HubSpot's assumption is that a capable non-technical person can administer it. Mostly true, and it is the real reason it wins in companies of this size. But it is still an assumption about a person, and "the marketing coordinator picked it up" is a plan that ends when the marketing coordinator gets another job.

So the honest decision rule: **choose the system your actual named person can administer, not the one that matches your ambitions.** Ambition is not an administration plan.

If you have a genuine Salesforce admin, or the budget for one at somewhere north of $80,000 a year in most US metros, the ceiling argument becomes real and you should take it seriously. If you don't, buying the more capable system is buying capability you cannot reach.

## When the answer is neither

Sometimes it is, and this is the part nobody selling software will tell you.

If you have fewer than about 200 accounts, one person doing sales, and a deal process that fits in your head, a spreadsheet and a calendar reminder will outperform a badly maintained CRM for another year. Not because spreadsheets are good, but because the failure mode is visible. When a spreadsheet is out of date you can see it. When a CRM is out of date it still produces confident reports.

The signals that you've actually outgrown it are specific. Two or more people need to see the same pipeline. You're losing follow-ups, which is [the expensive failure](/blog/b2b-lead-follow-up-speed/) in any B2B operation. Or you genuinely cannot answer which marketing activity produced which customer, which usually turns out to be [a tracking problem rather than a software problem](/blog/ga4-conversions-not-tracking/) and is worth checking before you buy anything.

## The cost nobody prices

Migration is never the data. Data moves.

The cost is the eight months where half the company uses the new system and half keeps the old habits, reports disagree with each other, and nobody trusts either number. That period is expensive and it's the same length whichever product you pick.

Which is an argument for picking once and staying. Both of these will still be here, both will keep working, and the churn of moving between them is almost always more expensive than whatever difference you were chasing.

## Where I come out

For most small businesses reading this, HubSpot on a lower tier, administered by a named person, with the contact count modelled honestly.

Salesforce when you have the admin, the complexity, or a credible plan to be much larger soon, and Salesforce is a genuinely better long-run answer in that case rather than a consolation.

And neither, for another year, if the real problem is that nobody is following up on the leads you already have. No CRM has ever fixed that, and a lot of them have made it easier to not notice.
