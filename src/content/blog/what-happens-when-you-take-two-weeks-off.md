You already know the answer to this.

Not approximately. You can name the three things that would stop, in order, in about four seconds, and you were naming them while you read the title.

So the audit is already done. What is missing is not information, it is permission, and the rest of this is about what that list actually means, because the usual reading of it is wrong in a way that costs money.

Here is the version I would argue for. Everything that stops when you stop was never a system. It was you. And the uncomfortable part, which almost nobody says out loud, is that those are usually the exact things you describe as your strength when somebody asks what makes the business work.

## The pattern in the list is more useful than the list

Run through what reliably goes quiet. Outbound stops first. Follow-up on anything ambiguous stops, while follow-up on anything obvious continues. The content stops. Approvals stop, so three other people's work stops behind them. And the relationships that live in your phone rather than in a system go cold for two weeks and nobody notices because nobody else knows they exist.

Now look at what did not stop. The invoices went out. The scheduled jobs got done. The recurring work carried on. Somebody answered the phone.

The split is not between important and unimportant. It is between things that run on a rule and things that require a judgment call.

That is the whole finding, and it is more useful than any list of symptoms, because it tells you what kind of problem you have. You do not have a delegation problem. You have an undocumented-judgment problem, and those get solved differently.

## Why hiring does not fix it

The standard response to this list is to hire somebody, and it is the move most likely to produce a worse version of the same dependency.

Hand an undocumented judgment to a new person and one of two things happens. They make the calls differently from you, you notice, you start reviewing their calls, and now the bottleneck is you plus a salary. Or they come and ask you each time, which is the original situation with an extra step and a payroll line.

Neither of those is the new person's fault. You transferred a task and kept the rule in your head, and the rule was the job.

## Most of what you call judgment is a rule you never wrote down

This is the part worth doing something about this week, and it takes an hour.

You can document a task in an afternoon. You genuinely cannot document a judgment. But the useful discovery is that most of what owners call judgment turns out to be a rule nobody ever articulated, and rules are transferable.

Here is the test. Try to say what you would tell somebody, in one sentence, with a threshold in it.

"If an enquiry mentions a deadline inside three weeks, quote high or decline it."

"If they ask for references before they have told us the budget, they are comparing on price, so send the case study rather than the client list."

"Anything under four thousand dollars, no proposal, just a one-page scope and a date."

Those are rules. They were sitting in your head looking like experience, and the moment a threshold goes into the sentence they become something a competent person can execute without you.

Try it on the three things from your list. In my experience two of the three turn out to be rules, and the one that resists is the one that is genuinely yours for now. That one gets extracted later, by somebody sitting alongside you, and for the specific case of handing over sales I went through the mechanics in [when should I hire my first salesperson](/blog/when-to-hire-first-salesperson/), which is the only version of this transfer I have seen work reliably.

## What survives your absence is the business. What stops is your job

These are two different assets and people conflate them constantly.

The part that runs without you has a value independent of you. It can be handed to a manager, sold, inherited, or simply left alone for a month. The part that stops is your employment, and it is worth exactly as much as your continued willingness to turn up.

I am not going to quote you a valuation rule, because I went looking for an authoritative one this morning and could not get the official text, and I would rather tell you that than cite somebody's reproduction of it. But you do not need a ruling to see the shape of it. Ask what a buyer would actually be purchasing if they bought your company tomorrow and you left in ninety days, and the honest answer is: whatever is on the list of things that did not stop.

This matters even if you will never sell. The same split tells you the maximum you could ever hand to a hire, which means it tells you the ceiling on the company's size, because a business cannot grow past the number of judgment calls one person can make in a week.

## Run the test on purpose

Most owners run this experiment eventually. They run it during an illness, a family crisis, or a funeral, which is the worst possible time to discover anything.

So choose a date instead, and instrument it lightly.

Before you go, write down which three things you expect to stop. Seal it, metaphorically. While you are away, do not rescue anything that is not an actual emergency, which is the hard part and the entire point, because every rescue destroys the data you went away to collect. When you come back, read your prediction against what happened.

You will be right about two and wrong about one, and the one you were wrong about is the interesting one. Either something you thought depended on you turned out not to, which is a quiet piece of good news you can build on, or something you had never worried about fell over, which is the finding you could not have got any other way.

## Where I come out

I think most owners in this position are not short of insight. They have simply never been given a reason to convert a private certainty into an afternoon of writing down thresholds, and the private certainty is comfortable in a way that writing it down is not, because written rules can be disagreed with.

The blunt version, and I would say it to anybody who asked me directly: a business that cannot run for two weeks without you is not a business you own. It is a job you cannot resign from, and the longer it stays that way the more expensive leaving becomes.

Two weeks is also not very long. You could pick the date before lunch.
