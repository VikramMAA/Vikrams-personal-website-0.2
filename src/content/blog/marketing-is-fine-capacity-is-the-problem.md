Say a job takes your crew a week.

Booked 80 percent of the time, a new customer waits about four weeks before you start theirs. At 90 percent, nine weeks. At 95 percent, nineteen.

You never got fifteen percent busier between those three lines. The wait got five times longer, and nobody in the company did anything wrong.

That's not a management failure or a scheduling failure. It's arithmetic, it's been known since the 1950s, and almost nobody running a service business has seen it, which is why the business feels like it broke on a specific Tuesday rather than drifting there over a year.

Here's where I'm going with this. If any of that sounds like your shop, more leads will make your business worse, and the honest first piece of marketing work is to stop buying demand and start selecting it. That's a real plan with real deliverables, and it is almost never what gets briefed.

## Where those numbers come from, and what they aren't

Standard queueing theory, and it's not controversial. The average time a job spends in the system equals the service time divided by one minus utilization. [Berkeley's IEOR notes](https://aswani.ieor.berkeley.edu/teaching/FA13/151/lecture_notes/ieor151_lec19.pdf), [Raj Jain's course at WashU](https://classes.engineering.wustl.edu/~jain/cse567-17/ftp/k_31asq.pdf) and [MIT's manufacturing systems notes](https://dspace.mit.edu/bitstream/handle/1721.1/109525/2-854-fall-2010/contents/lecture-notes/MIT2_854F10_MM1queue.pdf) all derive the same result. Strip out the work itself and the waiting alone comes to utilization divided by one minus utilization, in job lengths. Four at 80 percent. Nine at 90. Nineteen at 95.

Now the honest part, because the simple version assumes one crew and random arrivals, and you probably have three crews and a seasonal pattern.

Running more crews is more forgiving at the same utilization, so the cliff sits closer to 100 percent. But variability makes it worse rather than better, and a service business has brutally variable job sizes: a half-day callout and a three-week install in the same queue. The multiplier moves. The shape does not. Delay climbs gently, and then it climbs vertically.

Which gives you the thing that actually matters, and it's the reason this catches people.

**There is no warning.** At 70 percent booked you have a healthy business with happy customers. At 90 percent you have exactly the same business, the same people, the same standards, and a reputation problem. Nothing in between tells you it's coming, because the pain isn't proportional to the load.

## How to tell this is you, and not a conversion problem

These are the symptoms I actually look for, and none of them appears in a report.

The turnaround you quote on the phone has quietly slipped from the one on your website, and nobody has updated the website because that would mean admitting it.

You're doing delivery work in the evenings and calling it being hands on.

The people who leave are your good ones. That's not bad luck. Your best people have the most options and the least tolerance for a week where everything is urgent.

Follow-up happens when a gap appears in the day rather than when the enquiry arrives.

And the one that settles it: somebody asks what your ceiling is in new customers per month, and you have to do arithmetic on the spot rather than say a number.

Be careful not to confuse this with its opposite. If your enquiries are going cold because nobody is calling them back properly, that's a handling problem, and I went through [why home service leads don't turn into jobs](/blog/home-services-booking-rate/) in detail there. This post is about the other case: enquiries handled correctly, by a business with nowhere to put them. The arithmetic for working out your actual headroom sits in [what percentage of revenue should go to marketing](/blog/marketing-budget-percentage-of-revenue/), so I won't run it again here.

## The enquiries you drop are the big ones

This is the part I'd most like you to sit with, because it changes what being at capacity costs you.

When you're underwater, triage stops being deliberate and starts being driven by what's quick to answer. The one-line job gets a same-day quote, because answering it takes four minutes. The large one needs a site visit, a scope conversation with someone who isn't sure what they want, and two uninterrupted hours of thinking you haven't had in two weeks. So it sits in the inbox, and after nine days it isn't yours any more.

So running at capacity doesn't cost you a straight percentage of your volume. It biases your mix downward, toward small and simple, which is also your worst-margin work with the most admin per dollar.

And then it compounds in the wrong direction. You're now at capacity on cheap work, which means less room for the good work, which means the next large enquiry also waits. Nothing in your numbers shows this, because revenue looks fine and the pipeline looks busy.

A business at capacity isn't choosing its clients. It's being chosen by whoever asked the simplest question.

## What marketing does instead

If you accept the diagnosis, the brief changes from volume to selection. Four pieces of work, and all four are marketing rather than operations.

**Raise the price.** It's the only lever that reduces demand and increases revenue in the same move, and at capacity the risk profile is inverted: losing the bottom fifth of your enquiries isn't the cost of raising prices, it's the point of raising prices.

**Narrow the offer in public.** Take the services you do badly or reluctantly off the website. It costs nothing, it's a marketing change with an operational effect, and it's the fastest way to stop attracting the work that clogs the queue.

**Publish the qualifying criteria.** Minimum project size. Geography. What you don't do. And your real lead time, which is the most under-used sentence in service business copy. "Currently booking from mid-January" disqualifies everybody who needed you next week and reassures everybody who didn't, and it does that before anybody wastes a phone call.

**Spend the same budget on fewer, better-fit enquiries.** Tighter targeting, higher intent, more specific copy. The number at the bottom of the invoice doesn't have to fall. The brief underneath it does.

A business running at capacity should spend on price and positioning before it spends another dollar on reach.

That advice is unfashionable for a structural reason, which is that nobody sells it. There's no monthly retainer in "charge more and turn down a third of your enquiries", so the people you'd normally ask aren't going to be the ones who say it.
