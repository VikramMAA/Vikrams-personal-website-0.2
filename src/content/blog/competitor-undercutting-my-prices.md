You lost the job on price.

You know that because the buyer told you so. And the buyer told you so because it is the only answer that ends the conversation politely.

Before you decide anything, go and find out what the winning quote actually covered. Ask the buyer; a surprising number will show you, especially the ones who already feel slightly bad about it. Or reconstruct it from the competitor's own published scope.

In most of the cases I have looked at properly, the competitor was not cheaper. They were selling something smaller that photographs the same from across the room.

If that is what is happening, you do not have a pricing problem. You have a description problem, and the fix is in your proposal rather than in your price list.

But sometimes they really are cheaper, deliberately and sustainably, and that is a different situation with exactly three available responses. Each one costs something real. And which one is correct turns out to be decided by arithmetic about your competitor's balance sheet, not by anything about how good you are.

## Cheaper, or smaller?

Four things to compare, and none of them is the number at the bottom.

What is included and what is excluded, written out side by side. Who actually does the work, by name and seniority. What happens when something needs changing, including how many rounds and what a fifth round costs. And how payment is staged, because a lower price paid entirely up front is a different product from a higher price paid on completion.

If three of those four come out in your favor, you are not losing on price. You are losing because the buyer could not see the difference, and I have written separately about [handling that in the conversation itself](/blog/how-to-handle-price-objections-without-discounting/).

What follows is for the other case.

## What the law says about this, which is less than you hope

Almost every owner in this situation has the same private thought: surely selling below cost to drive me out is illegal.

There is a name for it and a test for it, and the test is close to unpassable.

In [Brooke Group Ltd. v. Brown and Williamson Tobacco Corp.](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ll/usrep/usrep509/usrep509209/usrep509209.pdf), decided in 1993, the Supreme Court set out what a plaintiff has to prove. There are two prerequisites: "(1) that the prices complained of are below an appropriate measure of its rival's costs and (2) that the competitor had a reasonable prospect of recouping its investment in below-cost prices."

Then the Court says the part that should settle it for you:

> "That below-cost pricing may impose painful losses on its target is of no moment to the antitrust laws if competition is not injured: It is axiomatic that the antitrust laws were passed for 'the protection of competition, not competitors.'"

And a few lines later, that "even an act of pure malice by one business competitor against another" is not enough on its own.

I am not your lawyer and your situation may have features I cannot see. The point is narrower and worth accepting quickly: the law is not interested in the fact that this hurts you. Spending another week on that thought is a week you could spend on the three things that are actually available.

## The Court's second test is the best business question you can ask

Here is where that case becomes useful rather than discouraging.

Look again at the second prerequisite. Did the competitor have "a reasonable prospect of recouping its investment in below-cost prices"? The Court sets out what that depends on: "the extent and duration of the alleged predation, the relative financial strength of the predator and its intended victim, and their respective incentives and will."

Now point that at your own market and ask it about them.

**Can they recoup?**

If they cannot, the low price is a temporary condition. They are funding it from somewhere, that somewhere will run out, and waiting is a legitimate strategy rather than a failure of nerve. Most small price-cutters in service markets fall here. They have underpriced out of inexperience or desperation, and in eighteen months they will either have raised their prices or stopped trading.

If they can recoup, you are not in a price war. You are in a market that has permanently changed shape, and waiting is the one thing guaranteed to lose. They can recoup if they have genuine scale advantages, a materially different cost base, outside funding with a long horizon, or if your entire category is a loss leader attached to something else they sell.

That question is about their cash. Almost every owner I have watched try to answer it instead answered a question about their own quality, which feels similar and tells you nothing.

## The three responses, and what each one costs

**Match them, and change your cost base to make it survivable.** The cost is that you have to actually change the cost base. Fewer hours per job, different tools, different people, a narrower deliverable. If you match the price without changing what it costs you to deliver, you are not competing, you are funding their strategy out of your own margin, and you will run out before they do.

**Hold your price and narrow who you sell to.** The cost is a smaller addressable market and a longer pipeline, and it only works if you publish the narrowing rather than just believing it. I went through the mechanics of doing that visibly in [the capacity post](/blog/marketing-is-fine-capacity-is-the-problem/), so I will not repeat them. The logic here is specific though: you cannot be both the cheapest and the best fit for everybody, and narrowing makes their comparison invalid rather than merely unflattering.

**Unbundle, so that a comparable cheap version of you exists on the shelf.** The cost is that you now run two products instead of one, and the cheap one will take some sales from the expensive one. That is not a reason to avoid it, but it needs to be priced in rather than discovered. On how to structure the cheap version so each option covers its own cost, the mechanism is in [which of your customers actually make you money](/blog/which-customers-are-actually-profitable/).

## Where I come out

For a business that genuinely cannot cut its costs, which is most small service firms, the answer is hold and narrow. Unbundle as well, but only if you can staff two things properly, because a badly run cheap tier damages the expensive one faster than the competitor ever could.

And the blunt version, which I would say to anybody in this position: matching a price you cannot sustain is not competing. It is agreeing to lose more slowly, with the added disadvantage that you have now told the market what your real price is.

One last thing, and it matters more than it sounds. Never tell a buyer the cheaper firm is bad. You do not know their cost structure, you may be wrong, and the buyer hears it as fear. Describe what is missing from the cheaper option in flat, neutral terms, say what that costs them if it goes wrong, and let them decide. The buyers who then choose the cheap option were never going to be your customers, and the ones who do not will remember that you did not sell by running somebody else down.
