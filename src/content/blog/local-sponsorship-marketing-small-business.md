The sponsorship request will not come from a salesperson.

It will come from a parent at your kid's school. From a customer who coaches on Saturdays. From the chamber president, who is also the insurance broker you have lunch with. From somebody standing in your showroom holding a folder.

That is the whole problem, and it has nothing to do with whether sponsorships work.

You cannot run a cost per impression analysis on a request from somebody you will see at a barbecue. So the evaluation does not happen, the check gets written, and twelve months later nobody can say whether any of it did anything. Which is then taken as evidence that sponsorship is unmeasurable, when what actually happened is that measuring it was socially impossible at the moment of decision.

The fix is structural rather than analytical. You decide the policy in January, when nobody is asking, so that in April you are answering with a policy instead of making a judgment about a relationship.

## The thing you are already treating it as

Here is a small clarifying point before the criteria, and it comes from the tax treatment.

If you receive something measurable in return, a logo on a kit, a banner, a mention in a program, the IRS framing treats that as advertising rather than as a gift. Several accounting sources describe it the same way: exposure received makes it an advertising expense. Where the payment mixes a real donation with advertising value, the split matters. I am not your accountant and the secondary sources on the details conflict, so ask yours.

But notice what that implies. You are already booking it as advertising. You are simply not evaluating it as advertising, which is the gap this post is about.

## The only mechanism that works

Local sponsorship has one route to revenue, and it is not awareness in general.

It is repeated exposure to a defined group of households who can already afford you, in a context where they are paying attention and feeling positively.

Each of those words is doing work. **Defined**, so you can name the group. **Already afford you**, which rules out a lot of worthy causes. **Repeated**, because one exposure does nothing. And **paying attention**, which is why a banner at a field where parents sit for two hours beats a logo on a flyer nobody reads.

Which makes the selection measurable with three questions, answerable before you commit:

How many of the right households will see it? Not attendance, the subset who could plausibly buy from you.

How many times, over how long? A season of Saturdays is a genuine media schedule. A one-day event is one impression.

And is your name attached to something they are already feeling good about, or is it on a list of twelve logos in six-point type?

If you cannot answer the first two with a number, you are making a donation. Which is allowed, and should be called that.

## Running the test on the usual options

**Team kit or uniform.** Usually the best of the common options for a local service business. The shirt is worn for a season, seen weekly by a repeating set of parents, and photographed. The arithmetic actually works, which is why it is the one I would do first.

**Field or facility banner.** Good if the venue is used by the same people repeatedly, poor at a tournament venue where the crowd rotates weekly. Same price, completely different buy, and nobody asks which one they are getting.

**Event booth.** This is a lead generation activity wearing a sponsorship costume, and it succeeds or fails on whether you planned the conversations. I went through that arithmetic for [B2B trade shows](/blog/are-trade-shows-worth-it-b2b/) and the logic carries down to a county fair.

**Chamber of commerce membership.** Not a sponsorship and not really advertising. It is a room, and its value is entirely in whether you work the room. The directory listing is worth close to nothing on its own, which is the same conclusion I reached about [BBB accreditation](/blog/is-bbb-accreditation-worth-it/) for similar reasons.

**Program ads and sponsor pages.** The logo-in-small-type category. I would decline these politely as a rule, because they fail the attention test entirely, and nobody has ever read a program's back page and then called a plumber.

## Negotiate for the thing nobody asks for

Here is the part that changes the economics, and it works because almost nobody tries it.

When you sponsor something, you negotiate over the logo. Size, placement, which shirt. The logo is the least valuable thing on the table.

What is valuable is access, and organizers will frequently say yes because it costs them nothing:

The email to parents or members. Not your advertisement, one message introducing you and what you do, sent by the organization rather than by you, which is a referral rather than a promotion.

A speaking or demonstration slot. Ten minutes at a meeting, a safety talk at a practice, a short session on whatever you genuinely know.

A table at the event rather than only a banner at it, so you have conversations instead of exposure.

And the right to say you sponsor them on your own site and vehicles, which is a trust signal in a local market and lasts longer than the season.

Ask for all four. You will get two. They are worth more than the banner, and the person asking you for money has never been asked for them before, which is why they are available.

## The policy, written now

The structural fix, and it takes twenty minutes once a year.

Write down the annual number you will spend on local sponsorship. Write down what you are trying to reach: the specific neighborhoods, schools, leagues or groups whose households are your customers. Write down that any commitment above a certain size needs the three questions answered in writing.

Then, when the ask arrives in April, the answer is not a judgment about the person in front of you. It is "our sponsorship budget for the year is committed, but ask me again in November when we set it."

That sentence is the entire value of the policy. It lets you say no without saying no to a neighbor, and it moves every decision to a month when you can think.

## The honest exception

Some of these you should do for reasons that are not marketing. Your child is on the team. You have lived here twenty years. The cause matters to you.

That is completely legitimate and I would not pretend otherwise. The only thing I would insist on is that it comes out of a different line, gets called a donation, and is never used as evidence that sponsorship does or does not work. Mixing the two is how businesses end up with a sponsorship budget that is really a goodwill budget and a conclusion that local marketing is unmeasurable.

## Before lunch

Twenty minutes, and it will save you the next four conversations.

Write the annual sponsorship number and the list of groups whose households you actually want.

Write the three questions on a card: how many of the right people, how many times, over how long.

Then write the November sentence, the one that lets you decline in April without declining a person, and tell whoever answers your phone what it is.
