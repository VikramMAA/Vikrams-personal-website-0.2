The email says you are live on the platform. Congratulations, catalogue approved, you are selling on quick commerce.

Before you tell anybody, do this. Open the app, change the delivery address to a pin code two kilometres from your best customer, and search for your own product.

There is a decent chance it is not there.

That is the gap between what the onboarding email means and what you assumed it meant, and it is the first of three operational things that decide whether any of this works. None of them is the listing, which is the easy part and the only part anybody writes about.

The strategic question of whether to be on these platforms at all is a separate argument and I made it in [is quick commerce killing D2C brands](/blog/quick-commerce-vs-d2c-india/), including the fee structure and the listing fee arithmetic. This is what happens after you have decided yes.

## Being listed is not being stocked

Here is the mechanism, and it is the thing founders find most surprising.

Your catalogue is approved at the platform level. Your inventory sits in the platform's warehouses, which replenish individual dark stores. What a customer can buy is whatever that particular dark store holds, and assortment decisions are made store by store, not nationally.

So "live on Blinkit" means your product is permitted to appear. Whether it appears in Koramangala, in Andheri West and in Gachibowli depends on whether those specific stores were allocated your stock, and a new brand is typically in a small number of stores to start with.

Which gives you a check worth running weekly rather than once. Pick five pin codes where you know you have customers, search your product in each, and record what you find. That five-minute exercise is more honest than any dashboard you will be given, and it tells you the one thing that matters in month one, which is whether your availability matches your demand geography.

If the answer is no, that is the conversation to have, and it is a different conversation from the one about advertising.

## So the assortment negotiation is the real one

The listing is a form. The thing that decides your economics is the conversation with the category manager about which of your products go in, in which pack sizes, in which stores.

Most brands arrive at that conversation with nothing decided and let the platform choose. The platform then optimises for its basket, which is a rational thing for it to do and is not the same as optimising for your margin.

Three things to decide before you walk in.

**Which SKUs, and deliberately fewer than you want.** Your whole range in a few stores performs worse than three products in many stores, because depth of availability is what produces repeat orders and breadth just spreads your stock thin. Pick the three that a new customer would try first.

**Which pack sizes, built for the occasion.** This is not the channel-differentiation point about keeping your value pack off the app. It is simpler: what people buy here is a top-up, because they have run out of something at nine in the evening. The pack that wins is the one somebody buys when they need it now, which is usually smaller than the hero SKU on your own website. Look at what sells in your category on the app before you decide, not at your own best seller.

**What you can supply every week without thinking about it.** Not your maximum. Your comfortable number, in the worst week of the month, with your current production. Write it down before anybody asks you, because the answer you give under pressure in that meeting becomes a commitment.

## Fill rate is a promise you make before you know the demand

This is the part that catches people and it has a nasty shape.

Fill rate, as these platforms use it, is the share of ordered units you actually supply and keep available at a store. Miss it and the reported consequence is that your listing gets demoted in that pin code, which means fewer orders, which means your fill rate recovers on paper while your visibility does not.

Read that again, because the failure compounds in a way most operational problems do not. Under-supplying does not just cost you the orders you missed. It costs you the ranking that would have produced the next ones, and getting that back is slower than losing it.

The thresholds get quoted confidently and inconsistently. I have seen demotion described as starting around 80%, targets given as 95% or better, and advice to hold 93% on your main products before increasing ad spend. None of those is published by the platforms, so treat them as the shape of the thing rather than as a specification, and ask your category manager what they actually measure you on.

The practical conclusion is the same whichever number is right, and it is the opposite of what ambition suggests. **Under-promise the assortment.** A narrow range you can always supply beats a wide one you miss, because the narrow one builds ranking and the wide one destroys it. You can always add SKUs. Recovering a demoted listing in a pin code is harder than earning it was.

## The portals, the process, and the numbers I will not give you

The entry points, which are consistent across every account I could find: Blinkit onboards through its Seller Hub, Zepto through its Vendor Hub, and Swiggy Instamart through a brand partner application from its partner page.

What they ask for is also consistent. GST registration, a bank account, and a trademark certificate or at least a filed application. Products are listed against barcodes, so get your GTINs in order before you start rather than during.

And the shape of the process is reported the same way everywhere: an evaluation, commercial terms agreed with a category manager, catalogue setup, dark store integration, then a soft launch in a limited set of stores.

Now the numbers. Reported onboarding timelines range from about a fortnight to two months, and the sources disagree enough with each other that I would not plan a launch date on any of them. Reported fee structures differ by platform and by account, none of the three publishes a rate card, and the figures circulating come from agencies that onboard brands for a living. Ask for your own terms in writing and treat every published number as somebody else's deal.

One thing worth doing that nobody mentions: write down the commercial terms yourself, in your own document, the day they are agreed. Category managers move roles, and the person who inherits your account will not have the context the last one had.

## Before lunch

Forty minutes, and the first item applies whether you are live or still negotiating.

Check five pin codes where your customers actually are, search your product in each, and write down what you find. Repeat it next Wednesday so you have a trend rather than a snapshot.

Then write your three numbers on one page: the three SKUs you want in, the pack size for each, and the weekly volume you can supply in a bad week. Take that page into every conversation.

Then find your GTINs and your trademark paperwork, because the one thing guaranteed to delay you is the admin nobody assigned to anyone.
