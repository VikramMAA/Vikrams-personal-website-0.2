Most companies asking how to generate more B2B leads do not have a generation problem.

They have a decay problem. The same lead, from the same campaign, with the same copy and the same spend behind it, is worth several times more at minute five than it is at hour twenty four. Nothing about the lead changed. The only variable is how long it sat there.

That is an operations problem wearing a marketing costume, and it is why the honest answer to "how do I get more leads" is frequently "stop wasting the ones you have".

## The numbers, including the one everybody misquotes

You have seen the claim that responding within five minutes makes you twenty one times more likely to qualify a lead than waiting thirty.

It is a real finding and it comes from the MIT and InsideSales Lead Response Management study, which is now nearly two decades old. Everybody cites it as though it were published last year. I would rather tell you that than pass it off as fresh.

The current figures do the same work and they are more useful.

Only about 23% of B2B companies respond to a lead within five minutes. Around 42% take longer than a full day. The average response time across B2B sits somewhere near 42 hours.

Leads contacted inside five minutes close at roughly 32%, against about 12% for those contacted after 24 hours. Between two and three times the outcome, from the same enquiry.

And the number that explains the whole thing: something like 78% of customers buy from the first company that responds.

Read the gap between the 42 hour average and the five minute standard as what it is. This is an entire industry knowing the answer and not doing it, which means it is available to anybody who simply does.

## Why first response wins so decisively

The instinct is that the best proposal wins and speed is a tiebreaker. It works the other way round.

When somebody makes an enquiry they have a problem they have just decided to do something about. They have not yet decided what the solution looks like, who the credible vendors are, or what questions matter. The first person who reaches them gets to frame all three.

By the time you call the next morning, a competitor has already told them what to look for, and the criteria they will judge you against were written by somebody else. You are now answering a question that was designed to be answered by a different company.

**You are not competing on quality in that first hour. You are competing on presence, and the winner sets the exam.**

## What I see happening in Indian B2B, specifically

Three things, and these are my own observations rather than survey data.

**The enquiry arrives on WhatsApp and there is no process around it.** The contact form is not where most Indian B2B enquiries land. They arrive as a message on somebody's personal phone, which means there is no queue, no owner, no timer and no record. The most valuable inbound channel in the business is also the least managed one. Related: if you cannot see those enquiries in your numbers at all, that is [the tracking hole worth fixing first](/blog/ga4-conversions-not-tracking/), because you cannot manage a response time you cannot measure.

**The reply goes out the next working morning.** An enquiry at four in the afternoon gets answered at ten the following day. That is eighteen hours, comfortably inside the band where the research says you have already lost most of the advantage, and it feels perfectly reasonable to everybody involved because nobody is being lazy. It is simply how the day is shaped.

**The buyer wants peer validation before they will take a call.** This is the part imported playbooks get wrong. An Indian B2B buyer, particularly at an SME, wants to know who else like them is using you before they will give you thirty minutes. Not logos of large enterprises. Somebody their size, in their city or their sector, who they could plausibly ring.

Which means your first response should not be a calendar link. It should contain the proof they were going to go looking for anyway.

## What a good first response actually contains

Not a pitch, because they did not ask for one yet.

An acknowledgement that names the specific thing they asked about, so they know a person read it. One sentence of relevant proof, ideally a comparable customer or a number from similar work. One question that is genuinely useful to answer. That is it.

The goal of the first message is not the meeting. It is to be the one that arrived first and sounded like a human who had read the enquiry, because that is the one they reply to.

## Speed is an operations problem and gets treated as a motivation problem

Most attempts to fix this are exhortation. Somebody says in a meeting that we all need to be faster on leads, everybody agrees, and it holds for nine days.

The four changes that actually hold:

**Route enquiries to a phone, not an inbox.** Whatever the channel, the notification has to reach a device somebody is holding. Email is where leads go to wait.

**One named person owns the hour.** Not the team, because a lead owned by everyone is owned by nobody. A rota is fine. Ambiguity is not.

**Write the first response template and let people adapt it.** Three sentences, per the structure above. Templates are not the enemy of a human reply, they are what makes a human reply possible in four minutes.

**Put time to first human response on the weekly report.** Not average, because one instant reply hides ten disasters. Use the median and the worst case. A number on a report that somebody has to read out loud changes behaviour in a way that a values conversation does not.

## The honest counter-argument

Speed on a badly qualified lead is just faster waste.

If your enquiries are mostly people who cannot afford you or do not fit what you sell, then a five minute response produces a five minute disappointment. The definition problem comes first, and I went through [why the lead quality argument usually turns out to be a definition argument](/blog/why-leads-are-not-converting/) separately.

Speed is also not a substitute for a follow up sequence. The first hour decides who gets heard. The next three weeks decide who gets bought, and that takes a mix of email, LinkedIn and a phone call, spaced so it reads as persistence rather than pressure.

But the sequence only matters if you are in the conversation at all, and the first hour is what determines that.

## Where I would start

Go and find your last twenty inbound enquiries, wherever they actually arrived, including the WhatsApp ones. Write down the time in and the time of the first human reply.

Take the median. If it is longer than an hour you have found a larger and cheaper opportunity than any campaign you were about to run, and it costs no media budget at all.

Then decide who owns the next one, by name, before it arrives.
