Two agencies in Bengaluru quote Rs 1 lakh for the same scope of work.

One of them is roughly 40% more expensive than the other, and neither of them knows which.

Because the number on the proposal is not the price. The price is what reaches your bank account, and what survives after you have delivered the work, and between the quote and that number sits a negotiation you did not plan for, a tax treatment that depends on who the client is, a deduction at source, and a payment date nobody agreed to in writing.

Almost nobody in this market has calculated the second number. Which is why pricing arguments here are conducted entirely about the first one, where the least money is decided.

So before you decide whether to hold your rate or meet the cheaper competitor, work out what you are currently collecting per hour delivered. That calculation changes more pricing decisions than any amount of positioning advice, and it takes an afternoon with last year's invoices.

## The four things between the quote and the bank

**The negotiation you did not price for.** In this market, movement is expected. If your number has no room in it and you refuse to move, you lose deals you should have won. If it has no room and you move anyway, you have funded the discount out of delivery.

**The tax treatment, which depends on the client.** More on this below, because it is the one most people have never thought about as a pricing question at all.

**Deduction at source.** For professional fees, the client typically deducts tax at source before paying you, commonly at 10%. You get it back eventually. The money you do not have is still money you do not have for several months.

**The payment date and what never arrives.** Sixty days becomes ninety. The final tranche gets queried. A small proportion of every year's invoicing quietly never lands.

None of this is exotic and none of it is anybody's fault. The point is only that four separate deductions sit between the number you argue about and the number you live on, and the conversation about pricing almost never includes any of them.

**Your price is what you collect, divided by what you actually delivered.**

## GST makes the same number two different prices

Here is the pricing insight I would most like Indian service businesses to take from this, and it is genuinely about who you sell to rather than what you charge.

Most professional and consultancy services carry GST at the standard 18%. I am not your accountant, the structure was reformed in 2025, classification depends on your SAC code, and you should confirm your own position with a CA rather than with a blog.

But the mechanics matter for pricing. A GST-registered business client claims input credit on what you charge them. The tax is paperwork to them, not cost. Your Rs 1 lakh plus GST costs them Rs 1 lakh.

A client who is not registered, or who is buying as a consumer, has no credit to claim. The same engagement costs them Rs 1.18 lakh in real money.

So the identical quoted number is an eighteen percent price difference depending on who receives it. Which means two things worth acting on.

Price resistance from unregistered and smaller clients is often not resistance to your rate. It is a genuinely higher price, and no amount of value articulation changes the arithmetic.

And if you are choosing which end of the market to chase, GST-registered buyers are structurally easier to sell to at a real rate. That is not a reason to abandon small clients. It is a reason to stop concluding that your pricing is wrong when the signal is coming from the segment where your price is, in effect, highest.

## Negotiation is a line item, not an event

Decide your posture before the call, because improvising it is how founders end up giving away margin they needed.

Two defensible positions exist. Price with room in it and concede structurally. Or price tightly, hold, and accept a lower conversion rate in exchange for better clients.

The first is more common here and it is not dishonest, provided the room is real and the concession buys something. On how to move without simply dropping, [I have written about trading rather than discounting](/blog/how-to-handle-price-objections-without-discounting/) and will not repeat the mechanics.

What I will add for this market specifically: if you choose the second posture, say so out loud and early. "This is the number, I do not have a discount version of this" is a sentence Indian buyers hear rarely enough that it reads as either confidence or arrogance, and which it reads as depends entirely on whether you can explain what the number buys. Have that explanation ready in one sentence before you need it.

## The question the pushback does not answer

A caution, because I have made the opposite point before and both are true.

I have argued, on the evidence of Gong's call analysis, that [prospects who push hardest on price are more likely to close](/blog/how-to-handle-price-objections-without-discounting/) than the ones who nod along. I still believe that. Haggling means engagement.

It says nothing at all about whether the engagement will be profitable.

Those are two different questions and they get conflated constantly. Whether somebody buys and whether the work pays are separate, and the second one is decided by the scope you agreed at the number you accepted. A client who negotiated you down to a price that no longer funds the support the work needs is not a bad person, and they were a good prospect. The engagement is still going to be the one that eats your Fridays.

So the thing to protect in a negotiation is not the number by itself. It is the relationship between the number and what you committed to deliver for it.

## Raising it on clients you already have

The practical version, since this is where most of the available money sits.

Give proper notice, three months rather than three weeks, and put it in writing. Raise it on the anniversary of the engagement rather than at a random month, because an annual review reads as process and a mid-year letter reads as opportunism.

Do not justify it with your costs. Nobody has ever paid more because their supplier's expenses went up. Justify it with what the scope has become, which is almost always larger than what was agreed, and name the specific things you now do that were not in the original engagement.

And offer the alternative honestly: the old price for the originally agreed scope. Some clients will take it, and that is a perfectly good outcome, because you have converted a loss-making engagement into a smaller profitable one.

Some will leave. Expect it, and know in advance which ones you can afford to lose, because deciding that during the conversation is how firms end up holding a price they announced and then quietly not holding it.

## Where I come out

Go and calculate the real number. Last year's collected revenue, not invoiced, divided by the hours you actually delivered. Include the unbilled calls and the revisions.

Most people doing this for the first time find they are working for substantially less than their rate card says, and that the gap is concentrated in two or three clients.

Then price the next engagement knowing that, and stop comparing your quoted number to anybody else's, because theirs is not a price either.
