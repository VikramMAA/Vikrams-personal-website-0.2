Customer profitability gets treated as a finance exercise. The answer is an acquisition brief.

Once you know which shape of client makes money, you know what to go and buy. Not a vertical. A shape: the size, how they decide, how many people have to agree, how they pay.

And here's the trap, and why it's urgent.

The revenue list flatters your worst cohort.

So your case studies get written about those accounts, because they're the big names. Your testimonials come from them. Your targeting, your service page, the examples in your proposals, all of it quietly describes the clients who lose you money, because the list you trust most put them at the top.

You're spending your acquisition budget on more of the wrong cohort, and the document telling you to do it looks like a spreadsheet of good news.

The fix doesn't need a costing system. Two numbers, which is Kaplan's own simplified method: the loaded cost per hour of each group doing the work, and the time their activities take. His example: a support department at $70 an hour, a transaction taking 24 minutes, so it costs $28.

For a small firm that's an afternoon, plus two weeks of honest time logging per client, counting the parts nobody logs: the Slack monitoring, the internal meeting about their internal meeting, the proposal revision that was account management.

It'll be rough. Rough is fine: you're ranking, not filing accounts.

A firm that can't name its most profitable customer type doesn't have an acquisition strategy. It has an acquisition budget.

Can you name yours?
