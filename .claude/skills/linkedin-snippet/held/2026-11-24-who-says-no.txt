Somebody in your company did say no to that customer.

It was your delivery lead, on a Thursday, six weeks after you'd said yes, in an email that opened with "just to set expectations".

By then the client had already given their own board the date.

The refusal happened. It always happens. The only variables are who says it and when, and nobody decided either of those on purpose.

Which is the thing worth fixing. The ability to decline is a function, like invoicing or hiring, and in most small companies nobody has been assigned it. So it defaults to whoever is closest to the work and latest in the process, which is the worst available combination.

And the cost isn't the scope creep. It's that the identical sentence carries opposite information depending on who says it and when.

"We don't do that, and here's why it wouldn't work for you", said by the person selling, before signature, is a standard. It reads as experience. It makes the rest of the proposal more credible, not less.

"We're not going to be able to do that part", said by the person delivering, in month two, is a shambles. It reads as a company that doesn't know what it sells.

Same content. The timing and the mouth are doing all the work.

So, the test. Name your last three refusals. Who said each one, when in the process, and to whom.

If you can't name three, you haven't been declining anything. Your limits still exist, because every company has them. They're being discovered by your customers in month two instead of published by you in week one.

Can you name three?
