# Hook archetypes, with worked examples

Seven shapes that work on LinkedIn for this kind of writing. Pick three different
ones per article so the user gets a real choice. Rotate them across days, because
the same opening three times running is what makes a feed read as automated.

The examples below are built from articles on this site. They are here for shape
and register, not to be reused verbatim.

## 1. The arithmetic

Take a price, a budget or a target the reader already has, and divide it into
something uncomfortable. This is the strongest shape available because the reader
does the sum on their own numbers while reading, which is dwell time you cannot
buy with a hook.

From "How much does a digital marketing agency cost?" [US]:

> Between $1,500 and $10,000 a month is what a US agency costs most small and mid-sized businesses.
>
> That number tells you almost nothing on its own, so do this instead.
>
> Take the retainer and divide it by a blended $150 an hour, which is roughly where US agency rates land once you average SEO, paid search and social.
>
> A $3,000 retainer is twenty hours.
>
> Now ask how many channels those twenty hours cover, and how much of it goes to the monthly report and the two status calls. Four channels means about four hours each. Four hours a month keeps something ticking over. It does not improve it.
>
> The useful question was never "how much". It's "how many hours, and whose". A $5,000 retainer where a senior person spends fifteen hours is a completely different product to a $5,000 retainer where a junior spends thirty five, and both get sold with the same deck.
>
> Anyone quoting seriously will tell you if you ask them.
>
> If you're on a retainer right now, do you know the hours behind it?
>
> Full breakdown in the comments.
>
> #DigitalMarketing #MarketingBudget #AgencyLife #B2BMarketing

Why it works: the answer is fully given, the reader is doing division on their own
retainer by line four, and the closing question is one a practitioner can answer in
six words.

## 2. The correction

Name the received wisdom, then say it's wrong and show the mechanism. Traction here
comes from people who half agree, which is the most comment-generating state there
is. It only works if the correction is defensible, so the mechanism has to be in
the post.

From "Is Google Ads worth it in a market as small as Estonia?" [Estonia]:

> Most Estonian B2B companies should not be running Google Ads domestically, and it isn't because the platform is bad.
>
> It's arithmetic.
>
> Target CPA needs roughly 30 conversions in a trailing 30 days before Google's bidding beats a person setting bids by hand. Target ROAS wants 50. Below about 15 a month, campaigns tend to run 35% to 50% higher cost per acquisition than campaigns clearing 30.
>
> Now put a country of 1.3 million people against that number.
>
> If your landing page converts at 3%, thirty conversions means a thousand clicks a month. Thirty three clicks a day on your keywords, every day.
>
> For a niche B2B product searched in Estonian, that volume often does not exist. Not "is expensive to reach". Does not exist. No budget buys clicks nobody is making.
>
> The version that does work is the same account pointed outward. Finland, Sweden, Germany, the US. The query volume is there and the automation finally has something to learn from.
>
> What's your domestic to export split on paid right now?
>
> #GoogleAds #Estonia #PPC #B2BMarketing

## 3. The scene

Two lines of something specific that a reader recognises as their own week, then the
point. Best for articles built on a behaviour rather than a number. Keep the scene
under three lines, because a scene that runs long turns into the fake-vulnerable
LinkedIn story and loses everyone.

From "WhatsApp stops being free in October" [India]:

> Send the same enquiry to an Indian business two ways.
>
> The contact form gets a reply in eleven hours, if it gets one. The WhatsApp message gets a reply in four minutes, usually with a voice note and a question back.
>
> That's why WhatsApp became the actual sales channel while the website turned into a brochure generating form fills nobody answers.
>
> What changes on 1 October is the money. Utility and service messages sent inside the 24 hour service window become chargeable, at about Rs 0.1150 each. The free replies you have been sending stop being free.
>
> For most businesses that is small. For anyone running support at volume, or sending three follow ups where one would do, it is a line item that appears out of nowhere.
>
> Two weeks is enough time to count how many of those messages you actually need to send.
>
> Has anyone here already modelled what October does to their number?
>
> #WhatsAppBusiness #DigitalMarketingIndia #LeadGeneration #SMB

## 4. The flat answer

The article title is a search query, so open with the answer to it and nothing else.
One short line, then the justification. Works when the answer is blunt enough to feel
like a stance: "Domestically, for most Estonian companies, no." "Between $1,500 and
$10,000 a month."

The risk is a one line hook with nothing under it. The second paragraph has to earn
the abruptness immediately.

## 5. The question people actually type

Open with the real question, phrased the way a person asks it out loud, attributed to
where it came from. "A founder asked me last month how much of a Rs 40,000 retainer
is actually work." Then answer it flat.

Use this sparingly and never invent the person. If nobody actually asked, use
archetype 4 instead.

## 6. The list that is actually useful

Three to five things the reader can do this week, written so they could act on it
without the article. Saves and shares come from this shape more than comments do,
which makes it a good post for a day when the article has no argument in it.

Rules: plain bullets, no emoji markers, no item that is filler to make the count
round, and one line of why under each rather than a bare imperative.

## 7. The mechanism

Explain why a thing happens rather than that it happens. Why impressions only ever go
up. Why a percentage of ad spend punishes efficiency. Why a traffic drop in week one
is usually not an algorithm update.

This is the shape that builds a reputation over months rather than getting a spike,
because a person who understands the mechanism is visibly different from a person
repeating advice. Slower on engagement, best on followers who matter.

# Anti-patterns

**Broetry.** Every line a single sentence with a blank line after it. It reads as
someone performing insight. Vary paragraph length like normal writing.

**The teaser.** "I wrote about why most agency retainers waste your money. Link in
comments." No answer in the post means no reason to stop, no comments, and less
traffic than a post that gave the whole thing away.

**The fake-vulnerable story.** A personal hardship that resolves into a marketing
lesson in the last line. Everyone can see the mechanism now and it reads as
manipulation.

**Engagement bait.** "Agree?" "Thoughts?" "Comment YES if you want the template."
"Repost if you found this useful." These attract the wrong comments and read as
someone farming a number.

**The stack of emoji bullets.** 🔹 lists. Immediately reads as scheduled content.

**Manufactured contrarianism.** "Unpopular opinion: SEO isn't dead." Take a position
that is actually contested and actually held, or take none.

**Anything that could be read as a pitch.** Covered in SKILL.md and it is the one
that matters most. "Here's how I'd fix it for you", "if this is your situation, my
DMs are open", "I've helped teams do exactly this". All out, no exceptions, including
in the first comment and in replies to comments.

**Naming a company as the bad example.** Criticise the pricing model, the practice,
the structure. Never a named business or a named person.
